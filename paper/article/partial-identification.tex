\section{Attainable labour ranges from interval observations}
\label{sec:sharp-identification}

Knowing how many person-hours a task took over two days can still leave its first-day labour uncertain. Which first-day totals fit the evidence? The \emph{identified range} contains precisely the labour totals compatible with the observations and each person's allocation limit in \eqref{eq:labour}. This definition follows the partial-identification approach in \cite[Section~1]{torgovitsky2019}. A range with a single value identifies the total exactly.

\subsection{Using the person's capacity}

Consider one person first. At most one person-hour of their labour can fit in an elapsed hour. If the time remaining after a cutoff cannot accommodate all their observed labour, the excess must fall before it.

Suppose this person worked $v$ person-hours between $\ell$ and $u$. At an intermediate time $c$, let $x$ be the labour accumulated so far. The remaining $v-x$ person-hours must fit between $c$ and $u$.\footnote{For $a:[\ell,u]\to[0,1]$, the value at $t$ is the fraction of the person's time spent on the work at that instant. Here $c$ denotes a time boundary. Proposition~\ref{prop:nonidentification} uses the same letter for a probability.}

\begin{proposition}[Attainable contribution from one interval]
\label{prop:sharp-cutoff}
Let $\ell<u$, $c\in[\ell,u]$ and $v\in[0,u-\ell]$. Among measurable allocations $a:[\ell,u]\to[0,1]$ with $\int_\ell^u a(t)\,dt=v$, the attainable values of $x=\int_\ell^c a(t)\,dt$ are exactly
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sharp-cutoff}
 \left[\max\{0,v-(u-c)\},\;\min\{v,c-\ell\}\right].
\end{equation}
If $v<0$ or $v>u-\ell$, no such allocation exists.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Nonnegativity and capacity on the two pieces imply
\[
 0\leq x\leq c-\ell,\qquad 0\leq v-x\leq u-c.
\]
Solving these inequalities for $x$ restricts it to the interval in \eqref{eq:sharp-cutoff}. Conversely, fix any $x$ in this interval. For $\ell<c<u$, define the allocation function by
\[
 a(t)=\begin{cases}
 x/(c-\ell),&\ell\leq t<c,\\
 (v-x)/(u-c),&c\leq t\leq u.
 \end{cases}
\]
Both allocation rates lie in $[0,1]$ by the preceding inequalities, so the function satisfies the capacity constraint. Its integrals over the two subintervals are
\begin{align*}
 \int_\ell^c a(t)\,dt&=\frac{x}{c-\ell}(c-\ell)=x,\\
 \int_c^u a(t)\,dt&=\frac{v-x}{u-c}(u-c)=v-x.
\end{align*}
The two integrals sum to $v$. At the endpoints the inequalities force $x=0$ for $c=\ell$ and $x=v$ for $c=u$. The constant allocation $a(t)=v/(u-\ell)$ realises both endpoint cases, including the zero integral over a subinterval of length zero. Since $0\leq a\leq1$, its integral $v$ must satisfy $0\leq v\leq u-\ell$.
\end{proof}

For example, suppose one person worked $3/2$ person-hours over two hours. At most one person-hour fits in the second hour, so at least $1/2$ person-hour falls in the first. The first hour has room for at most one. Using capacity narrows the range allowed by nonnegativity alone, $[0,3/2]$, to $[1/2,1]$.

When $v=u-\ell$, the person works throughout, up to sets of duration zero. Labour before $c$ then equals the time since $\ell$. More generally, for any subinterval $[b,c]\subseteq[\ell,u]$, apply the same argument to the subinterval and its complement. The attainable range is $[\max\{0,v-((u-\ell)-(c-b))\},\min\{v,c-b\}]$.

A person may divide the same hour between several tasks. The sum of the separate upper bounds also bounds total labour if each contribution is counted once. To attain the sum, however, one allocation must realise every component maximum. The corresponding condition for the summed lower bound is simultaneous attainment of the minima. Allocations over nonoverlapping intervals or for different people can be combined whenever no other constraint links them.

Suppose one person spent one person-hour on each of two tasks during $[0,2]$. Each task could account for anywhere from zero to one person-hour before time 1. Adding those ranges gives $[0,2]$. The two tasks filled both hours, so exactly one person-hour of their combined labour fell in the first. Figure~\ref{fig:shared-capacity} shows which first-hour allocations fit both task totals. The schedule in panel (c) attains the midpoint.

\subsection{Replacing allocation histories with interval totals}

To follow a person across tasks, call their work of one type on one task a \emph{stream}. Let $\mathcal I$ be the finite set of tasks, $\mathcal J$ the finite set of people and $\mathcal R$ the finite set of types of work. The tasks under study form a subset $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal I$. Choose a bounded calendar interval $[b_0,b_K]$, with $b_0<b_K$, containing every observation interval and target window. An admissible history consists of measurable $a_{ijr}\geq0$ with $\sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1$ almost everywhere for each person $j$. The sum limits the time that person can devote to all their streams.

Check that the study includes everyone who contributed to the target work and that its attribution rules cover their contributions. A task outside $\mathcal C$ can still occupy time the same person might otherwise have spent on a studied task. Include its stream when an observation of that work constrains the person's remaining capacity. Its hours contribute zero to the target.

Observation $\nu$, for $\nu=1,\ldots,M$, concerns an interval $[\ell_\nu,u_\nu]$ in this domain. Let $G_\nu\subseteq\mathcal I\times\mathcal J\times\mathcal R$ contain the streams it covers. The observation places finite bounds $v_\nu^-\leq v_\nu^+$ on their combined person-hours over that interval, so
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:interval-information}
 v_\nu^-\leq
 \sum_{(i,j,r)\in G_\nu}\int_{\ell_\nu}^{u_\nu}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt
 \leq v_\nu^+.
\end{equation}
For an exact interval total, $v_\nu^-=v_\nu^+$. A known rounding tolerance or a justified measurement-error limit can widen that interval. A Horvitz--Thompson estimate can vary with the sample, so its use here also requires bounds on sampling error.

The observations constrain integrals over intervals. To calculate all target totals compatible with them, we can retain the person-hours between successive boundaries.

Sort the distinct boundaries of the domain, observations and targets as $b_0<\cdots<b_K$. Each interval between successive boundaries is a \emph{cell}. Every observation and target now spans whole cells. For the productivity denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity}, include each studied task's tracking start $s_i$ and labour cutoff $c_i=s_i+\tau+\Delta$.

Express every boundary in calendar time first so tasks with different tracking starts draw on a person's capacity in the same cell. Keep cells outside a target window if an observation extends beyond it, since part of its labour could fall there. Write $d_k=b_k-b_{k-1}>0$ for the cell's duration in hours. The allocation fraction $a_{ijr}(t)$ for stream $(i,j,r)$ integrates to $x_{ijrk}$ person-hours over cell $k$. Target weights specify how much of each cell total enters the calculation.

\begin{proposition}[Exact cell reduction and attainable labour range]
\label{prop:cell-reduction}
Under the preceding model, define the finite-dimensional feasible set $\mathcal P$ by
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cell-capacity}
 x_{ijrk}&\geq0,&\sum_{i,r}x_{ijrk}&\leq d_k
 &&\text{for every }j,k,\\
\label{eq:cell-information}
 v_\nu^-&\leq
 \sum_{(i,j,r)\in G_\nu}
 \sum_{k:\,[b_{k-1},b_k]\subseteq[\ell_\nu,u_\nu]}x_{ijrk}
 \leq v_\nu^+ &&\text{for every }\nu.
\end{align}
Fix real weights $w_{ijrk}$ and define the target for a continuous-time allocation history by multiplying each cell integral by $w_{ijrk}$ and summing. The attainable target values over histories satisfying the capacity and observation constraints equal $h(\mathcal P)$, where $h(x)=\sum_{i,j,r,k}w_{ijrk}x_{ijrk}$. In particular, the denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity} uses $w_{ijrk}=1$ exactly when $i\in\mathcal C$ and $[b_{k-1},b_k]\subseteq[s_i,c_i]$, and zero otherwise. If $\mathcal P$ is nonempty, its target range is the closed interval
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sharp-labour-range}
 [H_{\min},H_{\max}]
 =\left[\min_{x\in\mathcal P}h(x),\;\max_{x\in\mathcal P}h(x)\right].
\end{equation}
Both endpoints and every value between them are attained, even if every $a_{ijr}(t)$ is restricted to $\{0,1\}$, so that each person does at most one type of work on one task at a time. If $\mathcal P$ is empty, the observations and capacity assumptions are inconsistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For any admissible history, the nonnegative allocation fractions integrate to nonnegative cell totals. Integrating one person's allocation limit over cell $k$ bounds their combined labour there by $d_k$, as required by \eqref{eq:cell-capacity}. Each observation interval consists of complete cells, so summing their integrals recovers its labour constraints in \eqref{eq:cell-information}. The target uses a constant weight within each cell, so its weighted integral equals the weighted sum of the cell totals.

Conversely, fix $x\in\mathcal P$. For each person and cell, order the task--type-of-work pairs and divide the cell into consecutive blocks whose lengths are their respective $x_{ijrk}$. The capacity constraint ensures that these blocks fit inside the cell. Use half-open blocks to avoid overlap at their boundaries. During each block, set the corresponding allocation to one and all others to zero. Leave any remaining time idle. Omit zero-length blocks. At the final domain endpoint, use the allocation values from the last block. These measurable allocations reproduce every cell labour total, so all observation bounds remain satisfied and the target value is unchanged. Each person works on at most one task and type of work at a time, with finitely many changes. Hence these restricted allocation histories attain every value of $h$ on $\mathcal P$.

The constraints define a closed convex set. Each coordinate lies in $[0,d_k]$, so a nonempty $\mathcal P$ is compact and its continuous linear target attains both extrema.\footnote{In finite dimensions a closed, bounded set is compact. By the extreme-value theorem, a continuous function attains its minimum and maximum on a nonempty compact set. Convexity means that $\eta x+(1-\eta)y$ belongs to the feasible set whenever $x$ and $y$ do and $0\leq\eta\leq1$. The linear target takes the corresponding weighted average of their values.} Along the line segment between a minimising and a maximising vector, the target takes every intermediate value. The block construction turns each vector on that segment into a schedule with allocations in $\{0,1\}$.
\end{proof}

To compute the endpoints, minimise the target in one linear programme and maximise it in another. Each uses $|\mathcal I||\mathcal J||\mathcal R|K$ variables, one capacity constraint for each of the $|\mathcal J|K$ person--cell pairs and $2M$ observation inequalities, as well as nonnegativity. To represent a known absence, include its start and end among the boundaries. Set the absent person's labour to zero in every cell of that period.

The construction uses at most $|\mathcal I||\mathcal R|+1$ constant blocks per person in each cell, including idle time. It allows the person to switch activity at any instant. Minimum session lengths, restrictions on interruption, task dependencies, smoothness requirements and switching costs each require further constraints or analysis.

Automated workflows may continue while the person attends to another task. The constructed schedule reproduces the observed labour totals. Establishing the actual sequence of work, acceptance times or output requires additional evidence. Where workflow restrictions rule out some schedules, the attainable totals still lie within \eqref{eq:sharp-labour-range}. Either endpoint may then be out of reach.

This replacement of fractional allocations by a single activity at each instant is a finite-partition instance of the Dvoretzky--Wald--Wolfowitz purification theorem \cite[Theorem~4]{dvoretzky1951ranges}.\footnote{For finitely many activities and a finite collection of atomless measures, purification preserves specified integrals while selecting one activity at each instant. Elapsed time on a cell is atomless because any measurable set of positive duration can be split into smaller sets of positive duration. Idle time accounts for any unused fraction.} For a general treatment see \cite[Section~2]{khanrathsun2006}.

\subsection{Checking the bounds and allowing observation error}

How can a reader check an endpoint reported by a solver? A feasible allocation with that total establishes that the person-hours could have occurred.

Optimality also requires a bound that applies to every feasible allocation. For a proposed maximum, an upper bound equal to the attained value suffices. A matching lower bound certifies a proposed minimum. The observation and capacity inequalities supply both checks.

Write the cell totals as a vector $x$, their target weights as $w$ and the constraints as $Ax\leq b$ with $x\geq0$. Reverse the sign of a lower observation bound to include it as a row.\footnote{Each row of $A$ contains the coefficients of one inequality. Its right-hand side is the corresponding entry of $b$. Vector inequalities compare corresponding entries. The superscript $\mathsf T$ denotes transpose, so $w^\mathsf T x$ is the weighted sum of the cell totals.} For nonnegative multipliers $\lambda$ and $\mu$, the following implication holds.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bound-certificates}
 A^\mathsf{T}\lambda\geq w,\qquad
 A^\mathsf{T}\mu\geq-w
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 -\mu^\mathsf{T}b\leq h(x)\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}b
 \qquad(x\in\mathcal P)
\end{equation}
For the upper bound, weight the first dual inequality by $x\geq0$ and the original constraints by $\lambda\geq0$. Summing across coordinates yields $w^\mathsf{T}x\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}Ax\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}b$. For the lower bound, apply this argument to $-w$.

When $\mathcal P$ is nonempty, linear-programming strong duality guarantees multipliers that give exactly $H_{\min}$ and $H_{\max}$ \cite[Sections~5.2--5.3]{boydvandenberghe2004}. With rational input, a reader can use fraction arithmetic to check a proposed rational allocation and its multipliers. Feasibility and equality of their objective values certify that endpoint without rerunning the solver.

Keeping the partition and target fixed lets us test the effect of wider limits on observation error. Raising an upper bound or lowering a lower bound increases its entry in $b$. Write the revised vector as $b+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon\geq0$ and zero entries for unchanged constraints. The original multipliers enclose every newly feasible target total in
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relaxed-certificates}
 [-\mu^\mathsf{T}(b+\varepsilon),\;\lambda^\mathsf{T}(b+\varepsilon)].
\end{equation}
Only the right-hand sides have changed, so the same weighted inequalities apply \cite[Section~5.6]{boydvandenberghe2004}. Solving again can tighten the interval. The measurement procedure must justify the size of each permitted error.

When a sample supplies the observation bounds, they can miss the true totals. Suppose they cover every true observation total simultaneously with probability at least $1-\alpha$, where $0<\alpha<1$. On that event, a true history satisfying the allocation constraints supplies a feasible cell vector. Its target value therefore lies between the calculated endpoints. The probability that the interval covers the target is therefore at least $1-\alpha$, counting an empty feasible set as a failure.

Individual observation bounds can miss on different samples. To guarantee simultaneous coverage from individual bounds, choose them so their failure probabilities sum to at most $\alpha$. The union bound then limits the probability of any miss to $\alpha$, whatever the dependence between observations.\footnote{If $E_\nu$ denotes failure of bound $\nu$, then $\Prb(\bigcup_{\nu=1}^M E_\nu)\leq\sum_{\nu=1}^M\Prb(E_\nu)$.} The sampling design and measurement procedure must justify those probabilities.

The Lean~4 development uses mathlib \cite{demouraullrich2021,mathlib2020} to check the reduction from interval integrals to cell totals, including the capacity inequality for each person. It also checks that a schedule with at most one human activity at a time can preserve the observation totals and weighted labour targets. Section~\ref{sec:accounting} accounts for concurrent AI execution separately.

Further Lean proofs verify the bounds from supplied allocations and dual multipliers and the implication from simultaneous observation coverage to target coverage. The mathematical arguments above supply the existence of optimisers and matching multipliers and the bound on the number of blocks. Separate tests exercise the Rust solver. A formal proof that its code implements the Lean definitions remains open.

\subsection{From labour bounds to productivity}

Let $Q=\sum_{i\in\mathcal C}Q_i(\tau,\Delta)$ be a known nonnegative follow-up output score. If the labour denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity} has $H_{\min}>0$, the attainable productivity range is $[Q/H_{\max},Q/H_{\min}]$. Its smallest rate occurs at the largest feasible labour total because $Q/H$ is nonincreasing for positive $H$.

Suppose the observations bound the output score by $0\leq Q_-\leq Q\leq Q_+$ and again $H_{\min}>0$. The interval $[Q_-/H_{\max},Q_+/H_{\min}]$ encloses every possible rate. This enclosure is exact when every rate in it comes from a feasible output--labour pair. This holds, for example, if every pair in $[Q_-,Q_+]\times[H_{\min},H_{\max}]$ is feasible.\footnote{This condition concerns possible pairs. In a probability model a copula relates the marginal distributions to the distribution of the pair, as in Sklar's theorem \cite[Theorem~2]{haugh2016copulas}. Statistical independence concerns those probabilities.} The optimisation over labour holds each task's acceptance time and the fraction of its requirements still met in \eqref{eq:retention} fixed. The next examples show how evidence about simultaneous work, zero labour or a link between labour and output affects the reported rate.
