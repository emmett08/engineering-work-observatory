\section{Attainable labour ranges from interval observations}
\label{sec:sharp-identification}

An interval total can leave open when people worked within it. We seek the labour totals possible within the target windows under all observations and the allocation constraint in \eqref{eq:labour}. The \emph{identified range} contains exactly the totals obtainable by allocations satisfying these conditions. This definition follows the general partial-identification perspective \cite[Section~1]{torgovitsky2019}. We begin with one person's work in one interval, then account jointly for overlapping observations and shared time.

\subsection{Using the person's capacity}

One person can contribute at most one person-hour in an elapsed hour. Knowing who supplied an interval total therefore limits how much of that labour could have occurred before or after a specified time. A total across several people must be compared with their combined available time.

\begin{proposition}[Attainable contribution from one interval]
\label{prop:sharp-cutoff}
Let $\ell<u$, $c\in[\ell,u]$ and $v\in[0,u-\ell]$. Among measurable allocations $a:[\ell,u]\to[0,1]$ with $\int_\ell^u a(t)\,dt=v$, the attainable values of $x=\int_\ell^c a(t)\,dt$ are exactly
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sharp-cutoff}
 \left[\max\{0,v-(u-c)\},\;\min\{v,c-\ell\}\right].
\end{equation}
If $v<0$ or $v>u-\ell$, no such allocation exists.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Nonnegativity and capacity on the two pieces imply
\[
 0\leq x\leq c-\ell,\qquad 0\leq v-x\leq u-c.
\]
Together, these inequalities give \eqref{eq:sharp-cutoff}. Conversely, fix any $x$ in this interval. For $\ell<c<u$, define the allocation function by
\[
 a(t)=\begin{cases}
 x/(c-\ell),&\ell\leq t<c,\\
 (v-x)/(u-c),&c\leq t\leq u.
 \end{cases}
\]
Both allocation rates lie in $[0,1]$ by the preceding inequalities, so the function satisfies the capacity constraint. Its integrals over the two subintervals are
\begin{align*}
 \int_\ell^c a(t)\,dt&=\frac{x}{c-\ell}(c-\ell)=x,\\
 \int_c^u a(t)\,dt&=\frac{v-x}{u-c}(u-c)=v-x.
\end{align*}
Adding them gives total labour $v$. When $c=\ell$, the interval for $x$ forces $x=0$. When $c=u$, it forces $x=v$. In either case, use the constant allocation $a(t)=v/(u-\ell)$ on the complete interval, so the zero-length subinterval contributes zero without division by its length. Finally, integrating $0\leq a\leq1$ gives the necessary condition $0\leq v\leq u-\ell$.
\end{proof}

For one person, $3/2$ person-hours in a two-hour interval imply between $1/2$ and $1$ person-hours in its first hour. At most one person-hour can fall in the second hour, so at least half a person-hour must fall in the first. The first hour's own capacity supplies the upper bound. Capacity therefore narrows the range permitted by nonnegativity alone, $[0,3/2]$, while leaving the first-hour total and timing partly open.

If $v=u-\ell$, the total uses all the person's available time, so the labour before any time within it equals the elapsed time from its start. The same accounting extends to an interior subinterval $[b,c]\subseteq[\ell,u]$. Applying the construction to that subinterval and the time outside it gives every possible labour total in the subinterval, $[\max\{0,v-((u-\ell)-(c-b))\},\min\{v,c-b\}]$.

Combining contributions requires checking whether their separate extrema can occur together. Separately calculated bounds can be added when each contribution is counted once. Attaining the summed lower bound requires every component minimum together, and attaining the summed upper bound requires every component maximum together. Disjoint intervals of one person, or intervals belonging to distinct people, can be combined when no other constraints link them. Overlapping observations or several tasks competing for the same person's time require a joint calculation.

For example, suppose one person spends one person-hour on each of two tasks during $[0,2]$. Each task separately allows $[0,1]$ person-hours before time 1. Together, the tasks use all the person's available time on $[0,2]$, so exactly one person-hour must fall before time 1. Adding the separately calculated bounds would instead give $[0,2]$. Figure~\ref{fig:shared-capacity} shows how the shared capacity restricts the jointly feasible first-hour totals within the separately permitted ranges. \subsection{Replacing allocation histories with interval totals}

The joint calculation must retain the capacity link between tasks sharing a person's time. Call one person's work of one type on one task a stream. Fix finite sets of tasks $\mathcal I$, people $\mathcal J$ and types of work $\mathcal R$, a set of tasks to be studied $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal I$, and a bounded calendar-time domain $[b_0,b_K]$ with $b_0<b_K$ containing every observation interval and target window. An admissible history consists of measurable $a_{ijr}\geq0$ satisfying $\sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1$ almost everywhere for every person $j$.

The list of people and the rules for attributing time to tasks determine whose labour and which work enter this calculation. Whether the records cover the intended work requires measurement evidence. Work outside $\mathcal C$ can be recorded separately when it uses the same people's time without contributing to the labour total for $\mathcal C$.

Each observation constrains the labour of specified streams during one interval. For observations $\nu=1,\ldots,M$, specify an interval $[\ell_\nu,u_\nu]$ within this domain, a set $G_\nu\subseteq\mathcal I\times\mathcal J\times\mathcal R$ of streams whose labour the observation includes, and finite labour bounds $v_\nu^-\leq v_\nu^+$, in person-hours. These bounds impose
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:interval-information}
 v_\nu^-\leq
 \sum_{(i,j,r)\in G_\nu}\int_{\ell_\nu}^{u_\nu}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt
 \leq v_\nu^+.
\end{equation}
Exact interval totals use $v_\nu^-=v_\nu^+$. Justified limits on rounding or measurement error may instead give different lower and upper bounds. A Horvitz--Thompson estimate varies with the selected sample. Its sampling uncertainty must be accounted for before the estimate can justify either inequality.

Form the sorted distinct endpoints $b_0<\cdots<b_K$ from the domain boundaries, every observation endpoint and every target boundary, and call the intervals between successive endpoints cells. Every observation interval and target window is then a union of complete cells, allowing its labour to be written as a sum of cell totals. For the denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity}, target boundaries include the start of tracking $s_i$ and the end of the labour observation window $c_i=s_i+\tau+\Delta$ for every task in $\mathcal C$.

The partition uses calendar time so that simultaneous work by the same person shares one capacity constraint. Translate time since tracking began into calendar time before partitioning. Cells outside a target window must remain whenever an observation spans them, because some of the observed labour may have occurred outside the window. Put $d_k=b_k-b_{k-1}>0$ and let $x_{ijrk}$ denote the labour allocated to stream $(i,j,r)$ in cell $k$. The following result shows that these cell totals preserve exactly the possible target values.

\begin{proposition}[Exact cell reduction and attainable labour range]
\label{prop:cell-reduction}
Under the preceding model, define the finite-dimensional feasible set $\mathcal P$ by
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cell-capacity}
 x_{ijrk}&\geq0,&\sum_{i,r}x_{ijrk}&\leq d_k
 &&\text{for every }j,k,\\
\label{eq:cell-information}
 v_\nu^-&\leq
 \sum_{(i,j,r)\in G_\nu}
 \sum_{k:\,[b_{k-1},b_k]\subseteq[\ell_\nu,u_\nu]}x_{ijrk}
 \leq v_\nu^+ &&\text{for every }\nu.
\end{align}
Fix real weights $w_{ijrk}$ and define the target for a continuous-time allocation history by multiplying each cell integral by $w_{ijrk}$ and summing. The attainable target values over histories satisfying the capacity and observation constraints equal $h(\mathcal P)$, where $h(x)=\sum_{i,j,r,k}w_{ijrk}x_{ijrk}$. In particular, the denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity} uses $w_{ijrk}=1$ exactly when $i\in\mathcal C$ and $[b_{k-1},b_k]\subseteq[s_i,c_i]$, and zero otherwise. If $\mathcal P$ is nonempty, its target range is the closed interval
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sharp-labour-range}
 [H_{\min},H_{\max}]
 =\left[\min_{x\in\mathcal P}h(x),\;\max_{x\in\mathcal P}h(x)\right].
\end{equation}
Both endpoints and every value between them are attained, even if every $a_{ijr}(t)$ is restricted to $\{0,1\}$, so that each person does at most one type of work on one task at a time. If $\mathcal P$ is empty, the observations and capacity assumptions are inconsistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Integrating any such history over each cell gives nonnegative cell labour totals. Integrating each person's allocation limit gives \eqref{eq:cell-capacity}. Splitting each observation at the cell boundaries gives \eqref{eq:cell-information}. The weighted target total is unchanged because its weights are constant on cells.

Conversely, fix $x\in\mathcal P$. For each person and cell, order the task--type-of-work pairs and divide the cell into consecutive blocks whose lengths are their respective $x_{ijrk}$. The capacity constraint ensures that these blocks fit inside the cell. Use half-open blocks to avoid overlap at their boundaries. During each block, set the corresponding allocation to one and all others to zero. Leave any remaining time idle. Omit zero-length blocks and give the final domain endpoint the same allocation values as the last block. These measurable allocations reproduce every cell labour total, so all observation bounds remain satisfied and the target value is unchanged. Each person works on at most one task and type of work at a time, with finitely many changes. Thus even these restricted allocation functions give exactly the same possible target values as the finite feasible set.

The constraints define a closed convex set. Each coordinate is between zero and $d_k$, so a nonempty $\mathcal P$ is compact and a linear functional attains its extrema. Convex combinations of minimising and maximising cell vectors attain every intermediate value. Reconstructing each such vector by consecutive blocks supplies an allocation with values in $\{0,1\}$ for each intermediate total.
\end{proof}

This reduction makes the extreme target totals computable by linear programmes, each with $|\mathcal I||\mathcal J||\mathcal R|K$ variables, $|\mathcal J|K$ shared-capacity constraints and $2M$ observation inequalities, in addition to nonnegativity. A known period of unavailability can be included by adding its boundaries to the partition and setting that person's cell labour totals to zero within it.

The reconstruction realises fractional cell totals with one human activity at a time. It uses at most $|\mathcal I||\mathcal R|+1$ constant blocks per person in each cell, including idle time. The reconstruction requires work to be allowed to start, stop and change type or task at arbitrary times within a cell. Minimum session lengths, restrictions on interruption, task dependencies, smoothness constraints and switching costs require a separate analysis.

The constructed schedule is one history of human activity consistent with the observed interval totals and weighted labour targets. AI workflows may keep executing concurrently while the person changes task. The equivalence concerns the labour quantities constrained by the model. Reproducing the actual order of task execution, acceptance times or output would require further information and constraints. Allocations satisfying these extra restrictions form a subset of the model's feasible allocations. Their labour totals therefore lie between the calculated minimum and maximum, although attainability for the restricted workflow requires a separate analysis.

Preserving the integrals while replacing fractional allocations by one activity at a time is a finite-partition instance of the Dvoretzky--Wald--Wolfowitz purification theorem \cite[Theorem~4]{dvoretzky1951ranges}. See also \cite[Section~2]{khanrathsun2006}. The consecutive-block construction gives the required history directly and bounds its number of blocks.

\subsection{Checking the bounds and allowing observation error}

To verify a proposed maximum, give a feasible allocation that achieves it and derive an inequality from the observation and capacity constraints showing that every feasible target total is at most that value. To verify a proposed minimum, give a feasible allocation that achieves it and derive an inequality showing that every feasible target total is at least that value. The allocation establishes attainability, while the inequality establishes optimality. Collect the cell totals and target weights into vectors $x$ and $w$, and write the capacity and observation inequalities as $Ax\leq b$, with $x\geq0$ kept separate. A lower observation bound is included by reversing its sign. Nonnegative multipliers $\lambda$ and $\mu$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bound-certificates}
 A^\mathsf{T}\lambda\geq w,\qquad
 A^\mathsf{T}\mu\geq-w
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 -\mu^\mathsf{T}b\leq h(x)\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}b
 \qquad(x\in\mathcal P)
\end{equation}
give bounds by taking weighted sums of the original inequalities, yielding $w^\mathsf{T}x\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}Ax\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}b$, and likewise for $-w$.

For a nonempty $\mathcal P$, linear-programming strong duality ensures that suitable multipliers give exactly $H_{\min}$ and $H_{\max}$ \cite[Sections~5.2--5.3]{boydvandenberghe2004}. When the bound calculated from the multipliers equals the total achieved by a feasible allocation, together they prove that the total is the minimum or maximum. If the problem data, cell totals and multipliers are rational, these checks can use exact arithmetic without repeating the optimisation. A numerical solver's success flag alone does not supply this check.

With the partition and target fixed, widening observation limits changes only the right-hand sides of the inequalities. Increasing an upper observation bound or decreasing a lower one increases the corresponding entry of $b$. If these changes give $b+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon\geq0$ and zero entries for unchanged constraints, the original multipliers still bound every newly permitted target total by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relaxed-certificates}
 [-\mu^\mathsf{T}(b+\varepsilon),\;\lambda^\mathsf{T}(b+\varepsilon)].
\end{equation}
The same weighted sums of inequalities give these bounds, as in the sensitivity analysis of \cite[Section~5.6]{boydvandenberghe2004}. Reoptimising may give a smaller interval. The observation process must still justify the error limits.

Sampled observation limits require a coverage argument. Suppose random lower and upper bounds contain all true observation totals simultaneously with probability at least $1-\alpha$, where $0<\alpha<1$. If the true history satisfies the allocation constraints, its cell vector belongs to the resulting random feasible set on that event. Optimising then gives an interval containing the true target total with probability at least $1-\alpha$. An empty feasible set counts as a failure to cover.

The simultaneous-coverage condition requires an argument for the actual sampling and measurement process. Coverage of $1-\alpha$ for each observation separately does not establish the same probability of covering them all. By the union bound, marginal error probabilities whose sum is at most $\alpha$ suffice. The resulting interval has a sampling-coverage guarantee, rather than the exact-data interpretation of \eqref{eq:sharp-labour-range}.

A separate development in Lean~4, using mathlib \cite{demouraullrich2021,mathlib2020}, checks that interval integrals equal sums of cell totals and that integrating each person's allocation limit gives the cell-capacity constraint. It constructs schedules of human activity in which each person does at most one activity at a time, preserving the observation totals and weighted labour targets. Concurrent AI execution remains part of the separate computing-resource account described in Section~\ref{sec:accounting}. The formal development also checks the bounds from supplied allocations and dual multipliers, and the implication from simultaneous coverage of observation totals to coverage of the target total. The existence of minimising and maximising allocations and dual multipliers, and the bound on the number of blocks in the reconstruction above, remain outside this formal supplement. The Rust solver is tested separately. The supplement does not prove that the Rust solver's source code implements the Lean definitions.

\subsection{From labour bounds to productivity}

Use the labour range corresponding to the denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity}, and let $Q=\sum_{i\in\mathcal C}Q_i(\tau,\Delta)$ be the total output score at follow-up, known exactly. With $Q\geq0$ and $H_{\min}>0$, dividing this output by the largest and smallest possible labour totals gives the identified productivity interval $[Q/H_{\max},Q/H_{\min}]$.

When the output score is also uncertain, separate output and labour bounds leave open which pairs can occur together. Justified output-score bounds $0\leq Q_-\leq Q\leq Q_+$ give an interval $[Q_-/H_{\max},Q_+/H_{\min}]$ containing every feasible output-to-labour ratio. It is the exact range only when every ratio in it can be produced by an output total and labour total that are jointly permitted. It is sufficient for every pair in $[Q_-,Q_+]\times[H_{\min},H_{\max}]$ to be permitted. A lower labour bound of zero cannot be used as a divisor. The acceptance times and the recorded fractions of requirements still met in \eqref{eq:retention} remain fixed throughout the allocation optimisation.
