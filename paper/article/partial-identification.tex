\section{Attainable labour ranges from interval totals and bounds}
\label{sec:sharp-identification}

A labour total for an interval can leave the timing of that work unknown. For each period whose labour we want to calculate, called a target window, we seek every total compatible with the supplied interval labour totals or bounds and the allocation constraint in \eqref{eq:labour}.

Each \emph{interval observation} specifies a time interval, the people, tasks and types of work included, and either their exact labour total or bounds on it. This is the meaning of ``observation'' in the mathematical results below. The \emph{identified range} contains exactly the totals that allocations satisfying these constraints can produce. This definition follows the general partial-identification perspective \cite[Section~1]{torgovitsky2019}.\footnote{Partial identification retains all values compatible with the observations and assumptions. Point identification occurs when this set contains exactly one value. Here the values are labour totals compatible with the supplied interval totals or bounds and allocation limits. When interval labour bounds are calculated from a sample, coverage describes how often the resulting random bounds contain the true total over repeated samples.} We begin with one person's work in one interval, then account jointly for overlapping intervals and shared time.

\subsection{Using the person's available time}

How much of a known interval total must lie before a chosen time? Labour that exceeds the available time after that point must have occurred before it. One person can contribute at most one person-hour in an elapsed hour, so each part of the interval has a known capacity. A total across several people must be compared with their combined available time.

In the proposition, $\ell$ and $u$ are the interval's start and end, $c$ is the time dividing it, and $v$ is the known total labour in that interval. The unknown $x$ is the labour before $c$.\footnote{The notation $a:[\ell,u]\to[0,1]$ assigns an allocation fraction between zero and one to each time in the interval. Square brackets include both endpoints. The functions $\min$ and $\max$ select the smaller and larger of their listed arguments. In this section, $c$ is a time boundary. The same letter in Proposition~\ref{prop:nonidentification} denotes a probability.}

\begin{proposition}[Attainable contribution from one interval]
\label{prop:sharp-cutoff}
Let $\ell<u$, $c\in[\ell,u]$ and $v\in[0,u-\ell]$. Among measurable allocations $a:[\ell,u]\to[0,1]$ with $\int_\ell^u a(t)\,dt=v$, the attainable values of $x=\int_\ell^c a(t)\,dt$ are exactly
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sharp-cutoff}
 \left[\max\{0,v-(u-c)\},\;\min\{v,c-\ell\}\right].
\end{equation}
If $v<0$ or $v>u-\ell$, no such allocation exists.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Nonnegativity and capacity on the two pieces imply
\[
 0\leq x\leq c-\ell,\qquad 0\leq v-x\leq u-c.
\]
Together, these inequalities give \eqref{eq:sharp-cutoff}. Conversely, fix any $x$ in this interval. For $\ell<c<u$, define the allocation function by
\[
 a(t)=\begin{cases}
 x/(c-\ell),&\ell\leq t<c,\\
 (v-x)/(u-c),&c\leq t\leq u.
 \end{cases}
\]
Both allocation rates lie in $[0,1]$ by the preceding inequalities, so the function satisfies the capacity constraint. Its integrals over the two subintervals are
\begin{align*}
 \int_\ell^c a(t)\,dt&=\frac{x}{c-\ell}(c-\ell)=x,\\
 \int_c^u a(t)\,dt&=\frac{v-x}{u-c}(u-c)=v-x.
\end{align*}
Adding them gives total labour $v$. When $c=\ell$, the interval for $x$ forces $x=0$. When $c=u$, it forces $x=v$. In either case, use the constant allocation $a(t)=v/(u-\ell)$ on the complete interval, so the zero-length subinterval contributes zero without division by its length. Finally, integrating $0\leq a\leq1$ gives the necessary condition $0\leq v\leq u-\ell$.
\end{proof}

For one person, $3/2$ person-hours in a two-hour interval require between $1/2$ and $1$ person-hours in its first hour. The second hour can contain at most one person-hour, forcing at least half a person-hour into the first. The first hour's own capacity gives the upper bound. Capacity therefore narrows the range from $[0,3/2]$, which nonnegativity alone permits, while leaving the exact first-hour total and timing unresolved.

If $v=u-\ell$, the total uses all the person's available time, so the labour before any time within it equals the elapsed time from its start. The same accounting extends to an interior subinterval $[b,c]\subseteq[\ell,u]$. Applying the same allocation rule inside and outside that subinterval gives its full range of labour totals, $[\max\{0,v-((u-\ell)-(c-b))\},\min\{v,c-b\}]$.

Why can adding separate task bounds give too wide a range? Allocations that are possible separately may be incompatible when they share a person's time. Adding lower bounds and adding upper bounds still gives valid bounds on the combined total, provided each contribution is counted once. Reaching the summed lower bound requires all component minima in one feasible allocation. Reaching the upper bound requires all component maxima together. Disjoint intervals for one person, or intervals for different people, can be combined when no other constraints link them. Labour bounds for overlapping intervals, or for tasks sharing a person's time, require a joint calculation.

For example, suppose one person spends one person-hour on each of two tasks during $[0,2]$. Each task separately allows $[0,1]$ person-hours before time 1. Together, the tasks use all the person's available time on $[0,2]$, so exactly one person-hour must fall before time 1. Adding the separately calculated bounds would instead give $[0,2]$. Figure~\ref{fig:shared-capacity} shows how the shared capacity restricts the jointly feasible first-hour totals within the separately permitted ranges. Panel (c) gives one schedule for the marked midpoint. It illustrates how consecutive activities can preserve the required totals, as proved below.

\subsection{Replacing allocation histories with interval totals}

The joint calculation must count all tasks drawing on the same person's available time. Call one person's work of one type on one task an allocation stream. Fix finite sets of tasks $\mathcal I$, people $\mathcal J$ and types of work $\mathcal R$, and select the tasks to be studied as $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal I$. Choose a bounded calendar-time domain $[b_0,b_K]$, with $b_0<b_K$, containing every observation interval and target window.\footnote{The notation $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal I$ means that every studied task belongs to the full task set. The Cartesian product $\mathcal I\times\mathcal J\times\mathcal R$ contains every task--person--work-type triple. Vertical bars such as $|\mathcal I|$ count the members of a finite set. A sum indexed by a set includes exactly its members. Later, $h(\mathcal P)$ means all target values $h(x)$ obtained from permitted vectors of cell labour totals $x\in\mathcal P$.} An allocation history permitted by the model consists of measurable $a_{ijr}\geq0$ satisfying $\sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1$ almost everywhere for every person $j$.

The people included and the rules for assigning time to tasks determine whose labour enters the calculation and which work it covers. Applying the model to workplace measurements requires checks that those people and attribution rules cover the intended work. Work on tasks outside $\mathcal C$ can have its own interval labour totals or bounds. These constrain the time available for studied tasks without adding to the target labour total.

Each interval observation constrains the labour of specified streams during one interval. For observations $\nu=1,\ldots,M$, specify an interval $[\ell_\nu,u_\nu]$ within this domain, a set $G_\nu\subseteq\mathcal I\times\mathcal J\times\mathcal R$ of included streams, and finite labour bounds $v_\nu^-\leq v_\nu^+$, in person-hours. These bounds impose
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:interval-information}
 v_\nu^-\leq
 \sum_{(i,j,r)\in G_\nu}\int_{\ell_\nu}^{u_\nu}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt
 \leq v_\nu^+.
\end{equation}
Exact interval totals use $v_\nu^-=v_\nu^+$. Justified limits on rounding or measurement error can give different lower and upper bounds. An estimate from sampled intervals, such as the Horvitz--Thompson estimate in Section~\ref{sec:estimation}, varies with the selected sample. Account for its sampling uncertainty before using it to justify either inequality.

How can we account for every possible activity history? We divide time at the domain boundaries and every endpoint of an interval carrying a labour bound or defining a target window. Keeping the labour total within each resulting interval preserves exactly the information needed for these calculations.

Sort these distinct boundaries as $b_0<\cdots<b_K$. Call each interval between successive boundaries a cell. Every observation interval and target window then consists of complete cells, so its labour is the sum of their totals. For the productivity denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity}, include each studied task's tracking start $s_i$ and labour cutoff $c_i=s_i+\tau+\Delta$ among these boundaries.

Use calendar time for this partition so work on different tasks shares the appropriate capacity constraint whenever it uses the same person at the same time. Convert task ages to calendar times first. Include cells outside a target window whenever an interval with a supplied labour total or bound spans them. Part of that interval's labour may lie outside the target window. Write $d_k=b_k-b_{k-1}>0$ for the cell duration and $x_{ijrk}$ for the labour in stream $(i,j,r)$ during that cell. Each $x_{ijrk}$ is measured in person-hours, while $a_{ijr}(t)$ is an allocation fraction. The weights in the following result select or scale cell contributions to the target. A weight of one includes a contribution and zero excludes it.

\begin{proposition}[Exact cell reduction and attainable labour range]
\label{prop:cell-reduction}
Under the preceding model, define the finite-dimensional feasible set $\mathcal P$ by
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cell-capacity}
 x_{ijrk}&\geq0,&\sum_{i,r}x_{ijrk}&\leq d_k
 &&\text{for every }j,k,\\
\label{eq:cell-information}
 v_\nu^-&\leq
 \sum_{(i,j,r)\in G_\nu}
 \sum_{k:\,[b_{k-1},b_k]\subseteq[\ell_\nu,u_\nu]}x_{ijrk}
 \leq v_\nu^+ &&\text{for every }\nu.
\end{align}
Fix real weights $w_{ijrk}$ and define the target for a continuous-time allocation history by multiplying each cell integral by $w_{ijrk}$ and summing. The attainable target values over histories satisfying the capacity and observation constraints equal $h(\mathcal P)$, where $h(x)=\sum_{i,j,r,k}w_{ijrk}x_{ijrk}$. In particular, the denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity} uses $w_{ijrk}=1$ exactly when $i\in\mathcal C$ and $[b_{k-1},b_k]\subseteq[s_i,c_i]$, and zero otherwise. If $\mathcal P$ is nonempty, its target range is the closed interval
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sharp-labour-range}
 [H_{\min},H_{\max}]
 =\left[\min_{x\in\mathcal P}h(x),\;\max_{x\in\mathcal P}h(x)\right].
\end{equation}
Both endpoints and every value between them are attained, even if every $a_{ijr}(t)$ is restricted to $\{0,1\}$, so that each person does at most one type of work on one task at a time. If $\mathcal P$ is empty, the observations and capacity assumptions are inconsistent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Integrating any such history over each cell gives nonnegative cell labour totals. Integrating each person's allocation limit gives \eqref{eq:cell-capacity}. Splitting each observation at the cell boundaries gives \eqref{eq:cell-information}. The weighted target total is unchanged because its weights are constant on cells.

Conversely, fix $x\in\mathcal P$. For each person and cell, order the task--type-of-work pairs and divide the cell into consecutive blocks whose lengths are their respective $x_{ijrk}$. The capacity constraint ensures that these blocks fit inside the cell. Use half-open blocks to avoid overlap at their boundaries. During each block, set the corresponding allocation to one and all others to zero. Leave any remaining time idle. Omit zero-length blocks and give the final domain endpoint the same allocation values as the last block. These measurable allocations reproduce every cell labour total, so all observation bounds remain satisfied and the target value is unchanged. Each person works on at most one task and type of work at a time, with finitely many changes. Thus even these restricted allocation functions give exactly the same possible target values as the finite feasible set.

The constraints define a closed convex set. Each coordinate is between zero and $d_k$, so a nonempty $\mathcal P$ is compact and a linear functional attains its extrema. Convex combinations of minimising and maximising cell vectors attain every intermediate value. Reconstructing each such vector by consecutive blocks supplies an allocation with values in $\{0,1\}$ for each intermediate total.
\end{proof}

Compactness ensures that the minimum and maximum are reached. Convexity ensures that every total between them is possible.\footnote{A convex set contains every weighted average $\eta x+(1-\eta)y$ of two of its points $x$ and $y$, with weight $0\leq\eta\leq1$. Closedness means that limits of convergent sequences in the set also belong to the set. Boundedness places finite limits on its coordinates. In finite dimensions these two properties imply compactness by the Heine--Borel theorem. The extreme-value theorem then guarantees that the continuous weighted sum $h$ attains a minimum and maximum on a nonempty feasible set. Convexity supplies every intermediate target value, and the proof arranges consecutive activities to realise each as a schedule.}

We can therefore calculate the minimum and maximum target totals using linear programmes, each with $|\mathcal I||\mathcal J||\mathcal R|K$ variables, $|\mathcal J|K$ shared-capacity constraints and $2M$ observation inequalities, in addition to nonnegativity. A known period of unavailability can be included by adding its boundaries to the partition and setting that person's cell labour totals to zero within it.

Arranging work in consecutive blocks gives each person one activity at a time while preserving the cell totals. It uses at most $|\mathcal I||\mathcal R|+1$ constant blocks per person in each cell, including idle time. This arrangement assumes that work can start or stop at any time within a cell and that the person can switch tasks or types of work at any such time. Minimum session lengths, restrictions on interruption, task dependencies, smoothness constraints and switching costs require a separate analysis.

The schedule obtained by arranging consecutive blocks is one history of human activity consistent with the supplied interval totals and weighted labour targets. AI workflows may keep executing concurrently while the person changes task. The continuous-time history and cell totals describe the same labour quantities under the model's constraints. Reproducing the actual order of task execution, acceptance times or output would require further information and constraints. Allocations satisfying these extra restrictions form a subset of the model's feasible allocations. Their labour totals therefore lie between the calculated minimum and maximum, although a separate analysis is needed to establish which totals the restricted workflow can achieve.

Preserving the integrals while replacing fractional allocations by one activity at a time is a finite-partition instance of the Dvoretzky--Wald--Wolfowitz purification theorem \cite[Theorem~4]{dvoretzky1951ranges}.\footnote{For finitely many activities and finite atomless measures, purification replaces fractional allocations by a single activity at each time while preserving the specified integrals. Here elapsed time is atomless. Every measurable set of positive duration can be divided into parts of smaller positive duration. Including idle time makes the allocation fractions sum to one. The consecutive-block proof supplies this replacement directly for the paper's finite time partition.} See also \cite[Section~2]{khanrathsun2006}. The consecutive-block argument supplies the schedule directly and limits the number of blocks it uses.

\subsection{Checking the bounds and allowing error in interval labour totals}

How can a proposed minimum or maximum be checked? Give an allocation that satisfies every interval labour bound and each person's capacity constraint and achieves the proposed total. Then derive a matching bound that applies to every feasible allocation.

For a maximum, the bound must put every feasible target total at or below the proposed value. For a minimum, it must put every feasible total at or above that value. The allocation establishes that the value is attainable, and the matching bound proves that it is the optimum.

Collect the cell totals and target weights into vectors $x$ and $w$, and write the capacity inequalities and interval labour bounds as $Ax\leq b$, with $x\geq0$ kept separate.\footnote{Each row of $A$ contains one constraint's coefficients, and the matching entry of $b$ is its limit. Vector inequalities compare corresponding entries. The superscript $\mathsf T$ denotes transpose, so $w^\mathsf T x$ sums the products of matching weights and cell totals. The vectors $\lambda$ and $\mu$ give nonnegative weights to constraints. The arrow $\Longrightarrow$ denotes implication.} A lower bound on an interval labour total is included by reversing its sign. Nonnegative multipliers $\lambda$ and $\mu$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bound-certificates}
 A^\mathsf{T}\lambda\geq w,\qquad
 A^\mathsf{T}\mu\geq-w
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 -\mu^\mathsf{T}b\leq h(x)\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}b
 \qquad(x\in\mathcal P)
\end{equation}
give bounds by taking weighted sums of the original inequalities, yielding $w^\mathsf{T}x\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}Ax\leq\lambda^\mathsf{T}b$, and likewise for $-w$.

For a nonempty $\mathcal P$, linear-programming strong duality ensures that suitable multipliers give exactly $H_{\min}$ and $H_{\max}$ \cite[Sections~5.2--5.3]{boydvandenberghe2004}.\footnote{Weak duality gives a valid bound from each feasible choice of multipliers. Strong duality states that the best such bound equals the optimum of the original linear programme. Here feasibility and bounded cell totals give finite attainable optima, and linear-programming duality supplies matching multipliers. Rational numbers are ratios of integers. When the data and proposed solution are rational, their equalities and inequalities can be checked using exact fraction arithmetic.} When the bound calculated from the multipliers equals the total achieved by a feasible allocation, together they prove that the total is the minimum or maximum. If the problem data, cell totals and multipliers are rational, these checks can use exact arithmetic without repeating the optimisation. The allocation and multipliers therefore let us check the minimum or maximum reported by a numerical solver.

With the partition and target fixed, widening the bounds on interval labour totals changes only the right-hand sides of the inequalities. Increasing an upper labour bound or decreasing a lower one increases the corresponding entry of $b$. If these changes give $b+\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon\geq0$ and zero entries for unchanged constraints, the original multipliers still bound every newly permitted target total by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relaxed-certificates}
 [-\mu^\mathsf{T}(b+\varepsilon),\;\lambda^\mathsf{T}(b+\varepsilon)].
\end{equation}
The same weighted sums of inequalities give these bounds, as in the sensitivity analysis of \cite[Section~5.6]{boydvandenberghe2004}. Solving the optimisation again may give a narrower interval. Evidence about the labour measurements and any rounding must still justify the size of those error limits.

Bounds estimated from a sample require an argument about how often they contain the true totals. Suppose random lower and upper bounds contain all the true interval labour totals simultaneously with probability at least $1-\alpha$, where $0<\alpha<1$. If the true history satisfies the allocation constraints, its cell vector belongs to the resulting random feasible set on that event. Optimising then gives an interval containing the true target total with probability at least $1-\alpha$. An empty feasible set counts as a failure to cover.

This coverage claim concerns all interval labour totals together. It therefore needs a justification based on the actual sampling and measurement process. Coverage of $1-\alpha$ for each interval total separately does not establish the same probability of covering them all. The union bound supplies one such justification when the individual failure probabilities sum to at most $\alpha$.\footnote{For events $E_1,\ldots,E_M$, the union bound is $\Prb(\bigcup_{\nu=1}^M E_\nu)\leq\sum_{\nu=1}^M\Prb(E_\nu)$. The union denotes the event that at least one $E_\nu$ occurs. Taking $E_\nu$ to be failure of the labour bounds for interval $\nu$ limits the probability of any failure by the sum of the individual failure probabilities. This inequality holds regardless of dependence between the events.} The resulting interval has a sampling-coverage guarantee, rather than the exact-data interpretation of \eqref{eq:sharp-labour-range}.

A separate Lean~4 development using mathlib \cite{demouraullrich2021,mathlib2020} checks the steps from interval integrals to cell totals and from each person's allocation limit to cell capacity. It also checks schedules with at most one human activity at a time while preserving the supplied interval totals and weighted labour targets. Concurrent AI execution remains in the separate computing-resource account described in Section~\ref{sec:accounting}.

The Lean development further checks bounds from supplied allocations and dual multipliers, and the implication from simultaneous coverage of interval labour totals to coverage of the target total. Its scope excludes the existence of minimising and maximising allocations, the existence of matching dual multipliers and the limit on the number of schedule blocks stated above. The Rust solver is tested separately. Correspondence between its source code and the Lean definitions remains outside the formal proof.

\subsection{From labour bounds to productivity}

Use the labour range corresponding to the denominator in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity}. Suppose the total output score at the follow-up assessments, $Q=\sum_{i\in\mathcal C}Q_i(\tau,\Delta)$, is known exactly. With $Q\geq0$ and $H_{\min}>0$, dividing this score by the largest and smallest possible labour totals gives the identified productivity interval $[Q/H_{\max},Q/H_{\min}]$. The labour bounds appear in reverse order because dividing a fixed nonnegative score by more labour gives the same or a smaller productivity ratio.

When the output score is also uncertain, separate output and labour bounds leave open which pairs can occur together.\footnote{These bounds describe which output--labour pairs the supplied labour and output constraints permit, including any restriction linking the two quantities. A probability model would also give probabilities for ranges of paired values. In such a model, a copula combines the separate distributions of output and labour to describe their statistical dependence, as formalised by Sklar's theorem \cite[Theorem~2]{haugh2016copulas}. The present calculation uses feasible pairs directly. Allowing every pair in the two ranges is a condition on possible values, whereas statistical independence is a condition on their probabilities.} Justified output-score bounds $0\leq Q_-\leq Q\leq Q_+$ give an interval $[Q_-/H_{\max},Q_+/H_{\min}]$ containing every feasible output-to-labour ratio. It is the exact range only when every ratio it contains comes from an output and labour total allowed together by all the labour and output constraints. It is sufficient for every pair in $[Q_-,Q_+]\times[H_{\min},H_{\max}]$ to be permitted. A lower labour bound of zero cannot be used as a divisor. The labour optimisation varies allocations while holding acceptance times and the assessed fractions of requirements still satisfied in \eqref{eq:retention} unchanged.
