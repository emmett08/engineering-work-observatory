\section{A shortest-path solution for one allocation function}
\label{app:scalar}

For one person's work of one type on one task, let $0\leq a\leq1$ be the allocation function. The observations of Section~\ref{sec:sharp-identification} bound labour within each observed interval. Subtracting cumulative totals gives the labour between two times. We can represent a bound on that difference by an edge in a graph. Using the sorted time boundaries and cell lengths defined there, set $z_k=\int_{b_0}^{b_k}a(t)\,dt$ for the cumulative labour up to boundary $b_k$. Represent an inequality $z_q-z_p\leq w$ by a directed edge $p\to q$ of weight $w$.

The graph combines capacity limits with the observed interval totals. Adjacent time boundaries receive edges $k-1\to k$ of weight $d_k$ and $k\to k-1$ of weight zero, encoding the maximum available time and nonnegativity. Observation bounds on $[b_p,b_q]$ add edges $p\to q$ of weight $v^+$ and $q\to p$ of weight $-v^-$. An exact observed labour total $v$ therefore gives edge weights $v$ and $-v$. This is the standard difference-constraints representation \cite[Slides~6--11]{indyk2011}.

Why does a shortest path give a labour bound? Adding the inequalities along a path gives an upper limit on the difference between its final and initial cumulative totals. The path with the smallest sum of edge weights gives the tightest upper limit. A reverse path bounds the opposite difference and therefore gives a lower limit on the target labour.\footnote{Vertices represent time boundaries. A directed edge represents an upper bound on the change in cumulative labour between two vertices, and a path adds the weights of its edges. Thus ``shortest'' means the smallest total weight. A negative edge weight can encode a lower labour bound after its inequality is reversed. A negative cycle makes the constraints inconsistent because adding its inequalities would require zero to be less than or equal to a negative number.}

\begin{proposition}[Attainable cumulative bounds from graph distances]
\label{prop:graph-bounds}
The observations for this single allocation function admit an allocation if and only if this graph has no directed cycle of negative total weight. When such an allocation exists, let $D(p,q)$ be the shortest-path weight from $p$ to $q$. For any endpoint pair $p\leq q$, the identified range of $\int_{b_p}^{b_q}a(t)\,dt$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:graph-range}
 [-D(q,p),\;D(p,q)].
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Summing edge inequalities around a cycle gives $0$ at the left, so a negative cycle is inconsistent. Conversely, adjacent edges make every vertex reachable from every other. In the absence of negative cycles, all shortest-path weights are finite and $D(r,r)=0$. For any starting vertex $r$, the values $z_k=D(r,k)$ satisfy each edge inequality because $D(r,v)\leq D(r,u)+w$ for an edge $u\to v$ of weight $w$. Subtracting $z_0$ makes initial cumulative labour zero without changing differences. The inequalities for adjacent endpoints give cell labour totals in $[0,d_k]$. Allocations constant within each cell can produce these totals and satisfy the observations.

Summing inequalities along paths yields $z_q-z_p\leq D(p,q)$ and $z_q-z_p\geq-D(q,p)$. Using $p$ as the starting vertex gives an allocation achieving the upper bound. Using $q$ gives an allocation achieving the lower bound. Convex combinations of these allocations give every labour total between those bounds.
\end{proof}

The reference checker implements these bounds with exact rational arithmetic and an all-pairs shortest-path calculation. Given indexed, sorted time boundaries, graph construction and this calculation use $O((K+1)^3+M)$ arithmetic operations and $O((K+1)^2+M)$ storage.\footnote{Big-$O$ notation describes an upper bound on growth, ignoring fixed multiplicative constants. There are $K+1$ time-boundary vertices and $M$ observations. The leading terms here are cubic in the vertex count for arithmetic operations and quadratic for storage.} The cost of each arithmetic operation depends on how many bits are needed to represent the input fractions. The checker compares the bounds and the allocations attaining them against an independent enumeration of finite allocations, including overlapping interval observations and inconsistent inputs.

When allocations to several tasks or types of work share a person's time, use the joint formulation in Proposition~\ref{prop:cell-reduction}. The graph above represents one person's allocation to one type of work on one task. Its numerical examples check that calculation. Applying these bounds to workplace observations also requires empirical checks of how labour was measured.
