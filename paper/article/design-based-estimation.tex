\subsection{Predictions calculated before sampling}
\label{sec:model-assisted}

A code-change model can miss time spent reviewing a design or helping a colleague. A probability sample lets us estimate how many person-hours those prediction errors add up to. First predict labour for every interval. On the sampled intervals, subtract predicted hours from measured hours and weight each difference by the inverse of the interval's selection probability. Adding these weighted residuals to the predicted total gives the fixed-prediction difference estimator \cite[Section~2.3]{denishaddou2026}. Prediction-powered inference uses the same construction for a mean \cite[Section~1.4 and Appendix~B of the accompanying preprint]{angelopoulos2023}.

Partition each person's target observation period into non-overlapping intervals. Let $U=\{1,\ldots,M\}$, $M\geq1$, be the complete list, or sampling frame, of these person-intervals, held fixed across samples. For interval $m$, let $d_m>0$ be its length in hours and $a_m\in[0,1]$ its average allocation to the target work. Its labour is $y_m=d_ma_m$ person-hours, and the population total is $H=\sum_Uy_m$.\footnote{The sampling design specifies the probabilities of selecting the possible samples from this fixed frame. The inclusion probability $\pi_m$ is the probability that interval $m$ is selected. The indicator $\ind\{m\in s\}$ is one when it is selected and zero otherwise. A hat, as in $\widehat H$, marks an estimate. The subscript $s$ on $\E_s$ or $\Var_s$ means that the average or variance is taken over possible samples, with labour values and predictions held fixed.}

Retain a prediction $f_m$ in person-hours for every interval in the frame. Convert Standard Coding Hours to predicted person-hours before using them here, and state the conversion calculation and its evidential basis. Observed and predicted labour must measure the same quantity. Each prediction must also remain unchanged whichever correction sample is selected. Training on an independent earlier population can meet this condition when all predictions are calculated and held fixed before selecting that sample.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-prediction difference estimator]
\label{prop:difference-estimator}
For a probability sample $s\subseteq U$ with known first-order inclusion probabilities $\pi_m>0$, set $r_m=y_m-f_m$ and
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}
 =\sum_{m\in U}f_m+\sum_{m\in s}\frac{y_m-f_m}{\pi_m}.
 \label{eq:difference-estimator}
\end{equation}
If $y_m$ and $f_m$ are fixed under the design and every sampled labour value is observed accurately, then $\E_s[\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}]=H$, where $\E_s$ averages over the sampling design.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $I_m=\ind\{m\in s\}$. Linearity and $\E_s[I_m]=\pi_m$ give
\[
 \E_s[\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}]
 =\sum_Uf_m+\sum_U\frac{\E_s[I_m]}{\pi_m}(y_m-f_m)
 =\sum_Uy_m.
\]
\end{proof}

Each interval contributes its full residual in expectation, even when several intervals are sampled together. Setting $f_m=0$ gives the Horvitz--Thompson estimator \cite{horvitz1952},
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{m\in s}\frac{d_ma_m}{\pi_m}.
 \label{eq:ht}
\end{equation}
The observation must match the quantity being estimated. An interval label should give the fraction of that interval spent on the target work. If the prompt instead asks about a single instant, draw that instant uniformly within the selected interval. If the prompt leaves the activity up to that instant undisturbed and the engineer reports it accurately, its expected allocation conditional on the interval history equals $a_m$. Multiplication by $d_m$ then estimates interval labour without bias, although the random instant introduces another source of variance. Asking what the engineer is doing when they eventually reply changes the observation time.

\paragraph{Unanswered prompts.}
Keep the response status of every selected interval. A reported zero records an observed allocation. A missed prompt leaves that allocation unknown, and using only replies with the original selection weights can bias the labour total. For example, if engineers answer between incidents and miss every prompt during incident response, those weights cannot recover the missing incident hours.

One option is to review a random subsample of missed prompts \cite{nonresponsefollowup}. Let $A$ contain the initial replies from sample $s$, and select $B$ from $s\setminus A$ with known conditional probabilities $\eta_m=\Prb(m\in B\mid s,A)>0$ for each missed prompt. If both the initial replies and the selected follow-ups accurately describe their original intervals, use
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\rm follow}
 =\sum_U f_m+\sum_{m\in A}\frac{r_m}{\pi_m}
             +\sum_{m\in B}\frac{r_m}{\pi_m\eta_m}.
 \label{eq:followup}
\end{equation}
Conditional on $s$ and $A$, the last sum averages to the weighted residual total for all missed prompts. Averaging next over the initial sample proves unbiasedness, even when initial response depends on workload. The variance must include both draws. If selected follow-ups still lack allocations, report how the estimate changes as their labour varies from zero to the interval duration. These limits describe sensitivity to the missing labels within the realised sample. Inference for the full frame also requires allowance for sampling uncertainty.

\subsection{Precision depends on residuals and the sampling design}
\label{sec:difference-variance}

The precision of the corrected estimate depends on how much the weighted residual total varies across possible samples. The following variance applies when sampled interval totals are measured exactly. Let $\pi_{mk}=\E_s[I_mI_k]$ be the probability that both frame units are selected, including $\pi_{mm}=\pi_m$. The exact variance over repeated samples is
\begin{equation}
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}})
 =\sum_{m,k\in U}
 \frac{\pi_{mk}-\pi_m\pi_k}{\pi_m\pi_k}\,r_mr_k.
 \label{eq:difference-general-variance}
\end{equation}
Expanding the variance of $\sum_U I_mr_m/\pi_m$ gives this expression. Its calculation requires residuals from the entire frame. When every pair has known $\pi_{mk}>0$, an unbiased estimate uses the observed pairs,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat V_s
 =\sum_{m,k\in s}\frac{\pi_{mk}-\pi_m\pi_k}{\pi_{mk}}
             \frac{r_m}{\pi_m}\frac{r_k}{\pi_k}.
 \label{eq:design-variance-estimate}
\end{equation}
Each pair enters with probability $\pi_{mk}$, which cancels its extra inverse weight. A design that draws exactly one interval per shift gives every pair of distinct intervals in that shift zero chance of appearing together. Equation~\eqref{eq:difference-general-variance} still defines the variance, but its within-shift cross-products cannot in general be estimated without bias from these observations \cite{aronow2013}. To observe such pairs, draw at least two intervals uniformly without replacement in shifts that contain two or more intervals. If one prompt per shift is the practical limit, use a variance bound justified for that design.

For simple random sampling without replacement from $M\geq2$ units, let the fixed sample size satisfy $1\leq n\leq M$. Every size-$n$ subset has the same probability and $\pi_m=n/M$. Let $\bar r_U$ and $\bar y_U$ be the means of the residuals and labour values over the complete frame. Their finite-population variances are
\[
 S_r^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_U(r_m-\bar r_U)^2,
 \qquad
 S_y^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_U(y_m-\bar y_U)^2.
\]
Substituting the selection probabilities for this sampling design into \eqref{eq:difference-general-variance} gives
\begin{equation}
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}})
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)S_r^2,
 \qquad
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{HT}})
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)S_y^2.
 \label{eq:difference-srs-variance}
\end{equation}
The factor $1-n/M$ accounts for sampling without replacement and becomes zero when every unit is measured. To estimate the variance from the sample, let $\bar r_s$ be its mean residual and $s_r^2$ its residual variance. For $2\leq n\leq M$, an unbiased estimator of the design variance is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat V_{\mathrm{diff}}
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)s_r^2,
 \qquad
 s_r^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{m\in s}(r_m-\bar r_s)^2.
 \label{eq:difference-srs-variance-estimator}
\end{equation}
The identity $s_r^2=[n(n-1)]^{-1}\sum_{m<k,\,m,k\in s}(r_m-r_k)^2$ expresses sample variance through pairwise differences. Each pair has inclusion probability $n(n-1)/[M(M-1)]$, so $\E_s[s_r^2]=S_r^2$. A one-unit sample from a larger population cannot supply this variance estimate. Measuring every unit gives zero sampling variance, including in a single-unit population. Errors in the recorded allocations can still affect the labour estimate, so measuring every unit also requires checks of measurement accuracy.

For $n<M$, both variances have the same positive sampling factor. Predictions therefore improve precision exactly when $S_r^2<S_y^2$, meaning that the residuals vary less across intervals than the labour values themselves. An unbiased correction alone gives no assurance of this reduction.

For example, take $M=4$, $n=2$, and $y=(0,2,4,6)$. True total labour is $12$, and the Horvitz--Thompson variance is $80/3$. Holding $f=(1,3,3,5)$ fixed across samples reduces the variance to $16/3$. Holding $f=(6,4,2,0)$ fixed instead raises it to $320/3$. This second prediction ranks the intervals in reverse order of their labour, increasing residual variation and giving four times the variance of estimation without predictions. Both corrected estimators remain unbiased.

\subsection{Failure cases and implementation checks}
\label{sec:difference-contract}

Fitting the predictor on the correction sample can bias the total. For $M=2$, $n=1$ and $y=(0,2)$, suppose a fitted rule memorises the selected outcome and predicts zero for the other unit. Its observed residual is always zero, so the correction leaves the fitted total unchanged. The resulting difference estimate is $0$ or $2$, each with probability $1/2$, giving expectation $1$ rather than $H=2$. A predictor trained on the sampled units therefore needs its own unbiasedness argument for the actual sampling design.

Replacing negative estimates with zero can also change the estimate's average over possible samples. The estimate can be negative even though true labour is nonnegative. With $y=(0,0)$, $f=(10,0)$ and one uniformly selected unit, \eqref{eq:difference-estimator} is $-10$ or $10$ with equal probability. Replacing the negative estimate with zero changes the expectation from $0$ to $5$. Reporting the calculated value, including its sign, preserves the estimator in \eqref{eq:difference-estimator} and exposes an implausible result for investigation.

\paragraph{A ratio with an estimated denominator.}
Let $Q$ and $H$ be the cohort totals in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity}, with $H>0$. Even an unbiased, always-positive labour estimate can give a biased productivity ratio. Suppose two intervals contain one and three person-hours, and $Q=6$ reference units. Sampling one interval uniformly gives $\widehat H_{\rm HT}=2$ or $6$ with equal probability. The two estimated rates are $3$ and $1$, averaging $2$ units per hour. The cohort rate is $6/4=1.5$. When the calculated labour estimate is zero or negative, report the component estimates and leave the point ratio undefined.

To quantify uncertainty, test candidate rates $p\geq0$ through the contrast $Q-pH$. Let $C_p$ be a confidence interval for this contrast with coverage at least $1-\alpha$ under the actual joint design. Then $\{p\geq0:0\in C_p\}$ covers $Q/H$ with probability at least $1-\alpha$, since $Q-pH=0$ at the true rate \cite{vonluxburg2009}. A design-based interval using estimated variances needs the covariance term,
\[
 \widehat V(\widehat Q-p\widehat H)
 =\widehat V(\widehat Q)+p^2\widehat V(\widehat H)
       -2p\,\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\widehat Q,\widehat H).
\]
The sampling and assignment design determines which intervals, tasks and teams must be treated jointly. State the coverage justification for $C_p$. If the resulting set has no finite upper endpoint, report it with the component totals. A team deciding whether the rate exceeds a prespecified minimum $p_0$ can report $Q-p_0H$ in reference units. When $H>0$, evidence that the rate exceeds $p_0$ requires a lower confidence bound for this contrast above zero.

\paragraph{Implementation scope.}
The implementation keeps the estimate's calculated sign and preserves observed zeros. It records missing responses separately from intervals that were not selected. Inputs are rejected when required information is missing, when selected intervals lack allocation measurements or when supplied values are inconsistent. Its general-design calculation uses complete sampled allocations and reports a total-labour estimate. Equation~\eqref{eq:followup} describes a proposed extension. For simple random sampling, it also reports variance and standard error when the conditions above hold.\footnote{The estimated standard error is the square root of the estimated sampling variance. It expresses the sampling spread in person-hours. A confidence interval also needs a justification for how often the procedure covers the true total. A standard error alone supplies no particular coverage probability.}

These input checks establish whether supplied records satisfy the implementation's rules. Collection evidence must additionally show that the frame covers the intended work, that allocations are measured accurately and that the actual selection method matches the method used to calculate inclusion probabilities.

Exhaustive finite tests cover the examples and input validation, including complete censuses and single-unit populations. Claiming confidence-interval coverage still requires a separate justification under the sampling design.
