\subsection{Estimating total labour with predictions fixed before the draw}
\label{sec:model-assisted}

Code changes can help predict implementation time while leaving a design discussion invisible. The difference estimator corrects the predicted total using measured hours from a probability sample of intervals \cite[Section~2.3]{denishaddou2026}. Prediction-powered inference applies the same construction to a mean \cite[Section~1.4 and Appendix~B of the accompanying preprint]{angelopoulos2023}.

Partition each person's observation period into non-overlapping intervals. The sampling frame $U=\{1,\ldots,M\}$ comprises all $M\geq1$ such intervals. In interval $m$, a person who devotes a fraction $a_m\in[0,1]$ of its $d_m>0$ hours to the work contributes $y_m=d_ma_m$ person-hours. The total of interest is $H=\sum_U y_m$.\footnote{The sampling design gives the probability of each possible sample. Summing over the samples containing interval $m$ gives its inclusion probability $\pi_m$. A hat denotes an estimate. The subscript $s$ on $\E_s$ and $\Var_s$ denotes expectation or variance over repeated draws from the fixed frame, holding all labour values and predictions fixed.}

Before drawing the sample, fix a prediction $f_m$ in person-hours for each interval. For example, a model fitted to an independent set of earlier observations can supply all $M$ predictions. A model expressed in Standard Coding Hours requires a conversion supported by measurements of person-hours for the work in question.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-prediction difference estimator]
\label{prop:difference-estimator}
For a probability sample $s\subseteq U$ with known first-order inclusion probabilities $\pi_m>0$, set $r_m=y_m-f_m$ and
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}
 =\sum_{m\in U}f_m+\sum_{m\in s}\frac{y_m-f_m}{\pi_m}.
 \label{eq:difference-estimator}
\end{equation}
For fixed labour values and predictions, accurate measurement of every sampled allocation gives $\E_s[\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}]=H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $I_m=\ind\{m\in s\}$ equal one if the sample contains $m$ and zero otherwise. Its expectation is $\pi_m$, so
\[
 \E_s[\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}]
 =\sum_Uf_m+\sum_U\frac{\E_s[I_m]}{\pi_m}(y_m-f_m)
 =\sum_Uy_m.
\]
\end{proof}

In expectation each residual contributes once, whatever dependence the design induces between selections. Setting every $f_m=0$ gives the Horvitz--Thompson estimator \cite{horvitz1952},
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{m\in s}\frac{d_ma_m}{\pi_m}.
 \label{eq:ht}
\end{equation}
A contributor can report how much of a completed interval they spent on the target work. A shorter question asks about one instant within that interval. Let $A_m(t)$ denote the contributor's allocation to the work at elapsed time $t$. After the interval ends, draw $U_m$ uniformly on $[0,d_m]$, independently of the realised activity history. If the contributor reports $A_m(U_m)$ accurately, then
\[
  \E\!\left[d_m A_m(U_m)\mid A_m\right]
  =\int_0^{d_m} A_m(t)\,dt=y_m.
\]
The choice of instant adds sampling variance. Retrospective collection also requires evidence that the contributor can identify the activity at the selected time.

A live prompt can change the activity it measures. Suppose an engineer works on one task until a prompt arrives at a uniformly chosen time during the hour, then moves to another task. Every truthful reply about the instant before the prompt identifies the first task, so the estimate credits that task with a full hour. On average the engineer spent half an hour on it. Choosing the instant retrospectively removes this source of bias.

\paragraph{Unanswered prompts.}
An accurate reply of zero contributes zero hours. A missing reply leaves those hours unknown. If engineers miss every prompt while handling an incident, the original selection weights applied to their other replies omit the incident work.

Random follow-up of unanswered prompts can recover their contribution \cite{nonresponsefollowup}. Let $A$ contain the intervals in the initial sample $s$ for which contributors supplied allocations. From $s\setminus A$, draw a follow-up sample $B$ with known conditional inclusion probability $\eta_m=\Prb(m\in B\mid s,A)>0$ for each missed interval. If each selected follow-up produces an accurate account of the original interval, and the initial replies are accurate, the two-stage estimator is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\rm follow}
 =\sum_U f_m+\sum_{m\in A}\frac{r_m}{\pi_m}
             +\sum_{m\in B}\frac{r_m}{\pi_m\eta_m}.
 \label{eq:followup}
\end{equation}
Conditional on $s$ and $A$, the last sum averages to the weighted residual total for all missed prompts. Averaging over the initial draw then gives $H$, even when workload influences the initial response. Both draws contribute to the sampling variance. If a contributor selected for follow-up still cannot supply an allocation, that interval may contain anywhere from zero hours to its full duration. Substituting the respective limits for every unresolved interval bounds the estimate for this particular sample. An interval for the population total must also allow for variation across samples.

\subsection{Precision depends on residuals and the sampling design}
\label{sec:difference-variance}

With exact measurements, only the weighted residual sum varies across draws. Write $\pi_{mk}=\E_s[I_m I_k]$ for the probability that the sample includes intervals $m$ and $k$, with $\pi_{mm}=\pi_m$. The variance is
\begin{equation}
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}})
 =\sum_{m,k\in U}
 \frac{\pi_{mk}-\pi_m\pi_k}{\pi_m\pi_k}\,r_mr_k.
 \label{eq:difference-general-variance}
\end{equation}
This expansion of $\Var_s(\sum_U I_mr_m/\pi_m)$ includes residual products from the whole frame. If every $\pi_{mk}$ is known and positive, the sampled pairs give an unbiased variance estimate,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat V_s
 =\sum_{m,k\in s}\frac{\pi_{mk}-\pi_m\pi_k}{\pi_{mk}}
             \frac{r_m}{\pi_m}\frac{r_k}{\pi_k}.
 \label{eq:design-variance-estimate}
\end{equation}
Each pair appears with probability $\pi_{mk}$, which cancels its inverse weight in expectation. Sampling just one interval per shift gives $\pi_{mk}=0$ for distinct intervals in the same shift. Their residual products enter \eqref{eq:difference-general-variance}, although the design reveals at most one residual in each such pair. Unbiased estimation of those products is generally impossible \cite{aronow2013}. Drawing at least two intervals uniformly without replacement in each shift of two or more intervals makes every within-shift pair observable. A one-prompt-per-shift design needs a variance bound justified for that design.

Under simple random sampling without replacement, every subset of $n$ intervals from a frame of $M\geq2$ is equally likely, where $1\leq n\leq M$ and $\pi_m=n/M$. Let $\bar r_U$ and $\bar y_U$ be the frame means of residuals and labour respectively. Their finite-population variances are
\[
 S_r^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_U(r_m-\bar r_U)^2,
 \qquad
 S_y^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_U(y_m-\bar y_U)^2.
\]
Under this design, \eqref{eq:difference-general-variance} simplifies to
\begin{equation}
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}})
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)S_r^2,
 \qquad
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{HT}})
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)S_y^2.
 \label{eq:difference-srs-variance}
\end{equation}
With at least two sampled intervals, the sample residual variance $s_r^2$ estimates $S_r^2$ without bias. Writing $\bar r_s$ for the sample mean residual gives
\begin{equation}
 \widehat V_{\mathrm{diff}}
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)s_r^2,
 \qquad
 s_r^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{m\in s}(r_m-\bar r_s)^2.
 \label{eq:difference-srs-variance-estimator}
\end{equation}
The pairwise form of the sample variance is $[n(n-1)]^{-1}\sum_{m<k,\,m,k\in s}(r_m-r_k)^2$. Each pair occurs with probability $n(n-1)/[M(M-1)]$, giving $\E_s[s_r^2]=S_r^2$. One observation from a larger frame supplies no pair for this calculation. A census, including a census of one interval, has zero sampling variance because it observes the entire frame. Measurement error can still affect the hours reported.

For $n<M$, the same positive factor multiplies both variances. Predictions improve precision exactly when residuals vary less across the frame than labour itself, $S_r^2<S_y^2$.

Suppose a team samples $n=2$ of $M=4$ intervals containing $y=(0,2,4,6)$ person-hours. For the total of $12$ hours, the Horvitz--Thompson estimator has variance $80/3$ squared person-hours. The predictions $f=(1,3,3,5)$ reduce the variance to $16/3$. Predictions that rank the intervals in reverse order, $f=(6,4,2,0)$, raise the variance to $320/3$, four times the Horvitz--Thompson variance. Under either predictor the estimates still average to $12$ across all six samples (Figure~\ref{fig:sampling-spread}).

\subsection{Limits of the sampling calculation}
\label{sec:difference-contract}

Fitting a predictor on the correction sample can introduce bias. Take $M=2$, $n=1$ and $y=(0,2)$. A model that reproduces the selected value and predicts zero for the unobserved interval has a sampled residual of zero. Its estimated total is therefore either $0$ or $2$ with equal probability. These values average to $1$, although $H=2$. Reusing the correction sample for fitting requires a separate justification for unbiasedness.

Residual correction can yield a negative estimate. With $y=(0,0)$, $f=(10,0)$ and one uniformly selected interval, \eqref{eq:difference-estimator} returns $-10$ or $10$ with equal probability. The signed estimates average to the true value of zero. Replacing the negative value with zero raises their mean to $5$.

\paragraph{A ratio with an estimated denominator.}
Let $Q$ and $H>0$ be the cohort totals in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity}. Estimating $H$ without bias does not guarantee an unbiased ratio. Suppose two intervals contain one and three person-hours and the cohort produced $Q=6$ reference units. Selecting one interval uniformly yields $\widehat H_{\rm HT}=2$ or $6$ with equal probability. The corresponding rates of $3$ and $1$ units per hour average to $2$, although the cohort produced $6/4=1.5$ units per hour. When the labour estimate is zero or negative, report the two component totals without a point ratio.

For any candidate rate $p\geq0$, let $C_p$ be a confidence interval for $Q-pH$ with coverage at least $1-\alpha$ under the sampling design for both totals. At the true rate the contrast is zero, so the set $\{p\geq0:0\in C_p\}$ covers $Q/H$ with probability at least $1-\alpha$ \cite{vonluxburg2009}. The contrast variance depends on the covariance between estimated labour and output,
\[
 \widehat V(\widehat Q-p\widehat H)
 =\widehat V(\widehat Q)+p^2\widehat V(\widehat H)
       -2p\,\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\widehat Q,\widehat H).
\]
One sampled shift may contribute hours to several tasks. In a team-randomised experiment, those tasks also share the team's treatment. Both sources of dependence matter to coverage. If the confidence set for the rate is unbounded above, report that set with both totals. For a prespecified minimum rate $p_0$, a positive lower confidence bound for $Q-p_0H$ supports a rate above $p_0$ whenever $H>0$. The contrast has units of reference output.

\paragraph{Implementation scope.}
The labour-estimation command accepts measured zeros, retains signed estimates and checks the response status of each interval. It rejects inconsistent inputs or a missing allocation for a selected interval. Equation~\eqref{eq:followup} describes a possible extension for unanswered prompts. With complete replies, the command computes the weighted total. For simple random sampling it also reports the variance and standard error when the sample size permits.\footnote{Taking the square root of the estimated variance gives the estimated standard error in person-hours. A confidence interval requires a separate justification for its coverage.} For a general design it reports zero sampling variance in a census ($\pi_m=1$ for every interval). For other general designs, the command leaves the variance unavailable even if a particular draw includes the whole frame.

Finite tests enumerate the possible samples in the worked examples and exercise input validation through the single-interval case. Applying the estimator in a team requires evidence about who contributed, which intervals the design could select and how accurately contributors described them. The inclusion probabilities used for weighting must match the sampling procedure in the field.
