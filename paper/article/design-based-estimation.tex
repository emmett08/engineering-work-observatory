\subsection{Predictions calculated before sampling}
\label{sec:model-assisted}

A model based on code changes can miss the hours engineers spend reviewing designs or helping colleagues. A probability sample can estimate the total error in those predictions. Predict labour for every interval. For each sampled interval, divide the difference between measured and predicted hours by its selection probability. Add these weighted residuals to the predicted total to obtain the fixed-prediction difference estimator \cite[Section~2.3]{denishaddou2026}. Prediction-powered inference uses the same construction for a mean \cite[Section~1.4 and Appendix~B of the accompanying preprint]{angelopoulos2023}.

Divide each person's observation period into non-overlapping intervals. The sampling frame $U=\{1,\ldots,M\}$ contains all $M\geq1$ person-intervals and stays fixed across samples. Let $d_m>0$ denote the duration of interval $m$ in hours. If the person devotes a fraction $a_m\in[0,1]$ of that interval to the target work, the interval contributes $y_m=d_ma_m$ person-hours to the population total $H=\sum_Uy_m$.\footnote{The sampling design specifies a probability for each possible sample. To calculate the inclusion probability $\pi_m$ of interval $m$, add the probabilities of all samples that contain it. The indicator $\ind\{m\in s\}$ equals one for a selected interval and zero otherwise. A hat denotes an estimate. The subscript $s$ on $\E_s$ and $\Var_s$ denotes expectation and variance over possible samples, with the labour values and predictions fixed.}

Specify a prediction $f_m$ of the target labour for each interval, in person-hours. A model that returns Standard Coding Hours needs a conversion to person-hours. Specify how the conversion works and what evidence supports it for the target work. Fix all predictions before drawing the correction sample. One way is to fit the model on an independent, earlier population and save its predictions for the entire frame.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-prediction difference estimator]
\label{prop:difference-estimator}
For a probability sample $s\subseteq U$ with known first-order inclusion probabilities $\pi_m>0$, set $r_m=y_m-f_m$ and
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}
 =\sum_{m\in U}f_m+\sum_{m\in s}\frac{y_m-f_m}{\pi_m}.
 \label{eq:difference-estimator}
\end{equation}
If labour and predictions are fixed across samples and every sampled allocation is measured accurately, the estimate has sampling expectation $\E_s[\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}]=H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $I_m=\ind\{m\in s\}$. Since $\E_s[I_m]=\pi_m$, taking expectations term by term yields
\[
 \E_s[\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}]
 =\sum_Uf_m+\sum_U\frac{\E_s[I_m]}{\pi_m}(y_m-f_m)
 =\sum_Uy_m.
\]
\end{proof}

Each interval contributes its full residual in expectation, even when several intervals are sampled together. With $f_m=0$, the correction reduces to the Horvitz--Thompson estimator \cite{horvitz1952},
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{m\in s}\frac{d_ma_m}{\pi_m}.
 \label{eq:ht}
\end{equation}
An engineer can report the fraction of a sampled interval spent on the target work. A shorter prompt can ask about the activity at one instant drawn uniformly within that interval. Provided the prompt leaves the preceding work undisturbed and the engineer describes the selected instant accurately, the expected allocation at that instant equals $a_m$ conditional on the interval's activity history. The response multiplied by $d_m$ therefore estimates interval labour without bias. Its variance includes the random choice of instant. A delayed reply must still describe the selected instant.

\paragraph{Unanswered prompts.}
Keep the response status for each sampled interval. If an engineer replies that they spent no time on the target work, enter zero hours for that interval. An unanswered prompt leaves the allocation unknown. If engineers skip every prompt during incident response, weighting their other replies by the original selection probabilities will miss those incident hours.

A team can follow up a random subsample of unanswered prompts \cite{nonresponsefollowup}. Let $A$ be the subset of the initial sample $s$ that supplied allocations. Draw a follow-up sample $B$ from $s\setminus A$ so that each missed prompt has a known conditional inclusion probability $\eta_m=\Prb(m\in B\mid s,A)>0$. If replies at both stages accurately describe the original intervals, the estimate is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\rm follow}
 =\sum_U f_m+\sum_{m\in A}\frac{r_m}{\pi_m}
             +\sum_{m\in B}\frac{r_m}{\pi_m\eta_m}.
 \label{eq:followup}
\end{equation}
For fixed $s$ and $A$, the last sum has expectation equal to the weighted residual total for all missed prompts. The estimate therefore averages to $H$ over the initial draw, even if workload affects who replies. The variance must account for both draws. If any selected follow-ups still lack allocations, calculate the estimate first with all their labour values set to zero, then with each set to its interval duration. The resulting range shows how much the missing allocations can change this sample's estimate. Inference about the full frame must also account for sampling variation.

\subsection{Precision depends on residuals and the sampling design}
\label{sec:difference-variance}

With exact measurements of sampled interval labour, the corrected estimate varies only through the weighted residual total. Let $\pi_{mk}=\E_s[I_mI_k]$ denote the chance that the design selects both intervals $m$ and $k$, with $\pi_{mm}=\pi_m$. The variance across possible samples is
\begin{equation}
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}})
 =\sum_{m,k\in U}
 \frac{\pi_{mk}-\pi_m\pi_k}{\pi_m\pi_k}\,r_mr_k.
 \label{eq:difference-general-variance}
\end{equation}
The cross-products in this expansion of $\Var_s(\sum_U I_mr_m/\pi_m)$ involve residuals from the entire frame. When every $\pi_{mk}$ is known and positive, weighting each observed cross-product by the inverse probability of observing its pair estimates the variance without bias,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat V_s
 =\sum_{m,k\in s}\frac{\pi_{mk}-\pi_m\pi_k}{\pi_{mk}}
             \frac{r_m}{\pi_m}\frac{r_k}{\pi_k}.
 \label{eq:design-variance-estimate}
\end{equation}
A pair enters the sum with probability $\pi_{mk}$, cancelling its inverse weight in expectation. If the design draws one interval per shift, two distinct intervals in that shift have $\pi_{mk}=0$. Their residual products still enter \eqref{eq:difference-general-variance}. In general the sampled intervals cannot supply unbiased estimates of those products \cite{aronow2013}. To observe pairs, draw at least two intervals uniformly without replacement from each shift containing two or more. If the team can answer only one prompt per shift, use a variance bound justified for that design.

For simple random sampling without replacement from $M\geq2$ units, fix the sample size $1\leq n\leq M$. Every size-$n$ subset is equally likely, so $\pi_m=n/M$. Write $\bar r_U$ for the mean residual over the frame and $\bar y_U$ for the mean labour. Their finite-population variances are
\[
 S_r^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_U(r_m-\bar r_U)^2,
 \qquad
 S_y^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_U(y_m-\bar y_U)^2.
\]
Under this design, \eqref{eq:difference-general-variance} simplifies to
\begin{equation}
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}})
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)S_r^2,
 \qquad
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{HT}})
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)S_y^2.
 \label{eq:difference-srs-variance}
\end{equation}
The finite-population correction $1-n/M$ falls to zero in a census. Write $\bar r_s$ for the sample mean residual. For $2\leq n\leq M$, we can estimate the design variance without bias by substituting the sample residual variance $s_r^2$ for $S_r^2$,
\begin{equation}
 \widehat V_{\mathrm{diff}}
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)s_r^2,
 \qquad
 s_r^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{m\in s}(r_m-\bar r_s)^2.
 \label{eq:difference-srs-variance-estimator}
\end{equation}
The identity $s_r^2=[n(n-1)]^{-1}\sum_{m<k,\,m,k\in s}(r_m-r_k)^2$ expresses the sample variance through pairwise differences. Each pair has inclusion probability $n(n-1)/[M(M-1)]$, so $\E_s[s_r^2]=S_r^2$. A single observation from a larger frame supplies no pairs for this calculation. A census has zero sampling variance, including in a frame of one interval. Its labour total still depends on the accuracy of each allocation.

For $n<M$, both variances have the same positive sampling factor. The predictor improves precision exactly when its residuals vary less across the frame than measured labour does, $S_r^2<S_y^2$.

For example, sample $n=2$ intervals from a frame of $M=4$ with labour values $y=(0,2,4,6)$. Total labour is $12$ person-hours. The Horvitz--Thompson variance is $80/3$ squared person-hours. Using the fixed predictions $f=(1,3,3,5)$ reduces it to $16/3$. The predictions $f=(6,4,2,0)$ rank intervals in reverse order of labour. The resulting labour estimates have variance $320/3$, four times the variance without predictions. Both corrected estimators have expectation $12$.

\subsection{Limits of the sampling calculation}
\label{sec:difference-contract}

A predictor fitted on the correction sample can bias the labour estimate. Take $M=2$, $n=1$ and $y=(0,2)$. Suppose the predictor returns the observed value for the selected interval and zero for the other. Its sampled residual is always zero, so the estimated total equals the selected value. The two equally likely estimates, $0$ and $2$, average to $1$. The true total is $H=2$. Such a predictor needs a separate proof of unbiasedness under the sampling design.

Even when true labour is nonnegative, the corrected estimate can be negative. With $y=(0,0)$, $f=(10,0)$ and one uniformly selected unit, the estimate in \eqref{eq:difference-estimator} takes the values $-10$ and $10$ with equal probability. Replacing $-10$ with zero raises the estimate's expectation from $0$ to $5$. Keep the signed estimate when investigating the residual correction in \eqref{eq:difference-estimator}.

\paragraph{A ratio with an estimated denominator.}
Let $Q$ and $H$ denote the cohort totals in \eqref{eq:cohort-productivity}, with $H>0$. An unbiased estimate of labour can yield a biased productivity ratio even when every estimated denominator is positive. Suppose two intervals contain one and three person-hours, with total output $Q=6$ reference units. When one interval is sampled uniformly, $\widehat H_{\rm HT}$ takes the values $2$ and $6$ with equal probability. The corresponding rates of $3$ and $1$ units per hour average to $2$. The cohort rate is $6/4=1.5$. If estimated labour is nonpositive, report both component estimates and omit the point ratio.

To quantify uncertainty, test candidate rates $p\geq0$ through the contrast $Q-pH$. Let $C_p$ be a confidence interval for this contrast with coverage at least $1-\alpha$ under the design used to estimate both totals. Then $\{p\geq0:0\in C_p\}$ covers $Q/H$ with probability at least $1-\alpha$, since $Q-pH=0$ at the true rate \cite{vonluxburg2009}. Estimating the variance of the contrast requires the covariance between the estimated totals,
\[
 \widehat V(\widehat Q-p\widehat H)
 =\widehat V(\widehat Q)+p^2\widehat V(\widehat H)
       -2p\,\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\widehat Q,\widehat H).
\]
A single sampled shift can contribute hours to several tasks, and tasks from one team can share the same treatment. Use the dependence induced by sampling and treatment allocation when justifying the coverage of $C_p$. If the set of rates has no finite upper endpoint, report it alongside the two totals. A team can also test a prespecified minimum rate $p_0$. When $H>0$, a lower confidence bound above zero for $Q-p_0H$, measured in reference units, supports a rate above $p_0$.

\paragraph{Implementation scope.}
The implementation preserves signed estimates and measured zeros. It distinguishes unanswered prompts from intervals the design did not select. It rejects incomplete or inconsistent inputs, including a missing allocation for any sampled interval. The general-design calculation estimates total labour from complete sampled allocations. If every $\pi_m=1$, the full frame is the only possible sample, so sampling variance is zero. Other general designs return an estimate with variance marked unavailable, even if every interval happened to appear. Equation~\eqref{eq:followup} describes a proposed extension for unanswered prompts. For simple random sampling the command also reports variance and standard error under the conditions above.\footnote{The estimated standard error, measured in person-hours, is the square root of the estimated sampling variance. To attach a coverage probability to an interval, justify how often the sampling procedure produces an interval containing the true total.}

Exhaustive finite tests check the examples and input validation, including censuses and single-unit frames. A field study must establish which people and working hours the frame covers, check the accuracy of the allocations and verify that the selection probabilities used in the calculation match the procedure used to draw the sample.
