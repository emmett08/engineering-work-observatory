\subsection{Frozen predictions as auxiliary information}
\label{sec:model-assisted}

The difference estimator starts with a fixed prediction of total labour and corrects it using a probability sample of allocation observations. For each sampled person-interval, subtract predicted labour from observed labour and divide by the interval's inclusion probability. Under the conditions below, the sum of these weighted residuals averages over repeated samples to the true total minus the prediction total. Denis and Haddou give this fixed-prediction estimator and its variance under the sampling design explicitly \cite[Section~2.3, equations~(2)--(4)]{denishaddou2026}. Prediction-powered inference also identifies its estimator of a mean as a difference estimator and considers finite-population extensions \cite[Section~1.4 and Appendix~B of the accompanying preprint]{angelopoulos2023}.

Partition each person's target observation period into non-overlapping intervals. Let $U=\{1,\ldots,M\}$, $M\geq1$, be the complete, fixed frame of these person-intervals. For interval $m$, let $d_m>0$ be its length in hours and $a_m\in[0,1]$ its average allocation to the target work. Its labour is $y_m=d_ma_m$ person-hours, and the population total is $H=\sum_Uy_m$. For every frame unit, retain a prediction $f_m$ in person-hours. A raw SCH value requires a stated conversion to labour units before it can serve as $f_m$. Each unit's prediction must remain the same whichever sample is drawn. Training on an independent earlier population can satisfy this condition: hold all predictions fixed, then sample allocations to correct the predicted total.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-prediction difference estimator]
\label{prop:difference-estimator}
For a probability sample $s\subseteq U$ with known first-order inclusion probabilities $\pi_m>0$, set $r_m=y_m-f_m$ and
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}
 =\sum_{m\in U}f_m+\sum_{m\in s}\frac{y_m-f_m}{\pi_m}.
 \label{eq:difference-estimator}
\end{equation}
If $y_m$ and $f_m$ are fixed under the design and every sampled labour value is observed accurately, then $\E_s[\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}]=H$, where $\E_s$ averages over the sampling design.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $I_m=\ind\{m\in s\}$. Linearity and $\E_s[I_m]=\pi_m$ give
\[
 \E_s[\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}}]
 =\sum_Uf_m+\sum_U\frac{\E_s[I_m]}{\pi_m}(y_m-f_m)
 =\sum_Uy_m.
\]
\end{proof}

The residual correction makes the estimate design-unbiased even when the fixed prediction total differs from $H$. Taking $f_m=0$ gives the Horvitz--Thompson (HT) estimator \cite{horvitz1952},
\begin{equation}
 \widehat H_{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{m\in s}\frac{d_ma_m}{\pi_m}.
 \label{eq:ht}
\end{equation}
For every frame interval, record one of three states: selected with an observed allocation (including zero), selected with no recorded allocation value (nonresponse), or unselected. The proof concerns true allocation values. Unbiasedness in the presence of measurement error requires additional assumptions on that error.

\subsection{Precision depends on residuals and the sampling design}
\label{sec:difference-variance}

Let $\pi_{mk}=\E_s[I_mI_k]$ be the probability that both frame units are selected, including $\pi_{mm}=\pi_m$. The exact variance over repeated samples is
\begin{equation}
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}})
 =\sum_{m,k\in U}
 \frac{\pi_{mk}-\pi_m\pi_k}{\pi_m\pi_k}\,r_mr_k.
 \label{eq:difference-general-variance}
\end{equation}
Expanding the variance of $\sum_U I_mr_m/\pi_m$ gives this expression. The calculation depends on how often pairs of units are selected together, through the covariances of their inclusion indicators. The variance formula in \eqref{eq:difference-general-variance} also uses residuals for unsampled units, which are generally unknown. To estimate it, retain the actual sampling design and satisfy the chosen variance estimator's conditions on joint inclusion probabilities.

For simple random sampling without replacement from $M\geq2$ units, let the fixed sample size satisfy $1\leq n\leq M$. Every size-$n$ subset has the same probability and $\pi_m=n/M$. Write
\[
 S_r^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_U(r_m-\bar r_U)^2,
 \qquad
 S_y^2=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_U(y_m-\bar y_U)^2.
\]
The inclusion covariances in \eqref{eq:difference-general-variance} give
\begin{equation}
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{diff}})
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)S_r^2,
 \qquad
 \Var_s(\widehat H_{\mathrm{HT}})
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)S_y^2.
 \label{eq:difference-srs-variance}
\end{equation}
For $2\leq n\leq M$, an unbiased estimator of the design variance is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat V_{\mathrm{diff}}
 =\frac{M^2}{n}\left(1-\frac nM\right)s_r^2,
 \qquad
 s_r^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{m\in s}(r_m-\bar r_s)^2.
 \label{eq:difference-srs-variance-estimator}
\end{equation}
Indeed, $s_r^2=[n(n-1)]^{-1}\sum_{m<k,\,m,k\in s}(r_m-r_k)^2$. Each pair has inclusion probability $n(n-1)/[M(M-1)]$, so $\E_s[s_r^2]=S_r^2$. Selecting one unit from a population containing more than one unit does not supply this variance estimate. Measuring every unit gives zero sampling variance, including in a single-unit population. Measurement error remains a separate question.

For $n<M$, the difference estimator improves on HT exactly when $S_r^2<S_y^2$: the residuals must vary less across the population than labour itself. Consider $M=4$, $n=2$, and $y=(0,2,4,6)$. The true total is $12$, and HT variance is $80/3$. Frozen predictions $f=(1,3,3,5)$ yield variance $16/3$, whereas $f=(6,4,2,0)$ yield $320/3$. Enumerating all six samples reproduces these variances and confirms that each estimator averages to the true total. It also verifies that \eqref{eq:difference-srs-variance-estimator} averages to each corresponding variance. The second predictor increases variance fourfold. The difference estimator remains design-unbiased with either predictor. These finite checks establish the calculations for these two predictors. A workplace study must determine whether its code-derived predictions give a residual total with lower sampling variance than the labour estimate without predictions.

\subsection{Failure cases and implementation checks}
\label{sec:difference-contract}

The proof above does not cover predictions fitted to the sample used for correction. For $M=2$, $n=1$ and $y=(0,2)$, suppose a fitted rule memorises the selected outcome and predicts zero for the other unit. Its observed residual is always zero. The difference estimate is nevertheless $0$ or $2$, each with probability $1/2$, giving expectation $1$ rather than $H=2$. A method trained on the sampled units needs a separate unbiasedness argument under the actual finite-population sampling design.

The estimate can also be negative even though true labour is nonnegative. With $y=(0,0)$, $f=(10,0)$ and one uniformly selected unit, \eqref{eq:difference-estimator} is $-10$ or $10$ with equal probability. Clipping at zero changes its expectation from $0$ to $5$. Preserve the signed estimate when reporting this estimator and investigate an implausible result. Dividing retained output by an unbiased labour estimate also need not yield an unbiased productivity estimate.

The accompanying implementation reports estimates with their calculated sign, preserves observed zeros, and records nonresponse separately from unselected units. It rejects declarations with missing required information, selected intervals without allocation measurements, and inconsistent inputs. For a general declared design it reports point estimates. For simple random sampling it also reports variance and standard error under the conditions above. These checks establish whether supplied records satisfy the input rules. Establishing that the frame covers the work, allocations are measured accurately and units were sampled by the declared method requires evidence from collection.

Exhaustive finite tests check the examples above, input validation, complete censuses and single-unit populations. A confidence interval requires a separate argument that its stated coverage holds under the sampling design.
