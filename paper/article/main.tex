\begin{abstract}
A repository can contain the same commit history for two engineers who devoted different amounts of time to the work. We derive the range of person-hours consistent with observed interval totals when several tasks draw on the same person's time. A finite linear programme gives both endpoints. With valid observations and unrestricted interruption, a schedule exists for every value between them.

We tested the calculation on all 96 histories in a constructed two-person population with known hours. With the same observations, enforcing the engineer's shared time limit reduced the mean width of the first-hour labour range from 1.5 to 0.5 person-hours. Omitting the reviewer excluded the true total in 26 histories. A second calculation estimates labour from a probability sample of intervals. Weighting accurately measured hours by the inverse of their selection probabilities gives an unbiased total for the covered intervals. Work outside that frame requires separate evidence. A workplace study would need to establish how much labour its measurements miss before using them to judge whether AI access saves human time on a fixed set of requirements.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the observations determine}
\label{sec:introduction}

Suppose an engineer accounts for three hours of work in a four-hour period. How much took place in the first two hours? At most two hours of work could fit in the second half, so at least one must fall in the first. The engineer could have worked for that one hour or for the whole first half. Both schedules account for all three hours. Narrowing the range requires evidence about when the engineer worked.

Commit times alone leave even the three-hour total unresolved. A release discussion may leave no commit. An artificial intelligence (AI) workflow may produce one while the engineer attends to another task. We define \emph{labour} as the time people devote to the included work, measured in person-hours. A team could reduce labour simply by abandoning more requirements. The proposed comparison therefore includes time to first acceptance and the fraction of each requirement still satisfied at follow-up if the team accepted it on time.

Perry et al. compared software developers' time diaries with direct observation \cite{perry1994}. When a team can place work within a period but cannot time each activity, the available hours still constrain the calculation. In preemptive scheduling, release times and deadlines divide the calendar into intervals of known processing capacity \cite{bertsimas2009flows}. We use the boundaries of observations about completed work. An observation can cover several tasks or contributors, and two observations can overlap. The least and greatest compatible person-hours show what these observations establish about the work in a chosen period. With sampled observations we use a generalised difference estimator \cite{cassel1976}. Predictions improve precision when the sample estimates their total error with less variance than it estimates total labour directly.

Wright and Ziegler's Standard Coder method \cite{wright2019} infers coding activity from commit times, then trains a second model to predict those activity labels from changes to the code. Accurate predictions of the labels establish how well the second model reproduces the first. Interpreting the predictions as person-hours requires independent measurements of labour. A change in how much work goes into each commit under AI assistance calls for a fresh comparison with those measurements. Forsgren et al.\ distinguish activity from efficiency in their account of developer productivity \cite{forsgren2021}. Mozannar et al.\ examine the human time spent interacting with AI-assisted programming tools \cite{mozannar2024}.

Published AI studies illustrate how the outcome and the people included in it affect the interpretation of a result. Peng et al.\ examine completion time among participants who finished a programming exercise \cite{peng2023}. Becker et al.\ study experienced maintainers in familiar repositories, comparing log completion times after adjustment for forecast times \cite{becker2025}. Cui et al.\ study weekly completed pull requests in organisational experiments \cite{cui2026}. In their June 2025 manuscript, they use allocation to study groups as an instrument for adoption \cite[Section 4.1 and Table 3]{cui2025manuscript}. A shorter time among finishers can coincide with fewer completions if more people abandon the exercise. A weekly pull-request count, in turn, depends on how teams divide the work into changes. Model Evaluation and Threat Research also describe difficulties selecting participants and measuring time when several agents work concurrently \cite{metr2026}. The examples below use illustrative values to show what each measure can establish.

Section~\ref{sec:observation} derives the commit distribution shared by two activity models. Section~\ref{sec:accounting} defines whose work counts and when observation ends. With those definitions in place, Section~\ref{sec:sharp-identification} derives labour bounds from interval totals, and Section~\ref{sec:estimation} estimates labour from sampled intervals. Section~\ref{sec:known-labour} tests both labour calculations on known work histories. Section~\ref{sec:study} examines measurement error, intake forecasts and a proposed workplace trial. Appendix~\ref{app:scalar} derives a shortest-path solution for the case of one person doing one type of work on one task.

\section{Why event telemetry alone need not identify labour}
\label{sec:observation}

The chance of a commit in a given minute depends on how often the engineer is active and how often an active minute produces a commit. If a smaller fraction of active minutes produce commits, more minutes of activity can yield the same distribution of commit times.

Let $Z_t=1$ when the engineer is active in minute $t$ and $Z_t=0$ otherwise. This activity is hidden from an observer who sees only $C_t$, the indicator that a commit occurs in that minute. A Bernoulli variable takes value one with its specified probability and zero otherwise.\footnote{Here $\Prb(A)$ denotes the probability of event $A$, and $\E[X]$ the expectation of $X$. The sum $\sum_{t=1}^{n}$ runs from the first minute to minute $n$. Each $Z_t\in\{0,1\}$ is binary. The activity probability $\pi\in(0,1)$ lies strictly between zero and one.} The model describes how activity varies over time and how it produces commits. Interpreting an active minute as a minute of coding requires an independent observation of what the engineer did.

\begin{proposition}[Failure of unrestricted identification]
\label{prop:nonidentification}
Suppose the activity indicators $Z_t$ are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter $\pi\in(0,1)$. Let $B_t$ be Bernoulli variables with parameter $c\in(0,1)$, independent across minutes and independent of the entire activity sequence $(Z_t)$. Define $C_t=Z_tB_t$. The probability of any finite sequence of commit observations depends on $(\pi,c)$ only through $\pi c$. In particular, $(\pi,c)=(1/2,1/5)$ and $(1/4,2/5)$ induce identical commit distributions with different expected active times.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each $C_t$ is Bernoulli with parameter $\theta=\pi c$, and the $C_t$ are independent. For every $n\geq1$ and every binary string $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:event-law}
 \Prb(C_1=e_1,\ldots,C_n=e_n)
 =\theta^{\sum_t e_t}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum_t e_t}.
\end{equation}
In both models, $\theta=1/10$. The expected number of active minutes depends directly on $\pi$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:activity-total}
 \E\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_t\right]=n\pi,
\end{equation}
which is $n/2$ minutes in the first model and $n/4$ in the second. The commit distribution therefore leaves expected activity ambiguous.
\end{proof}

In an hour, each model predicts six commits on average. The expected active time is 30 minutes in the first model and 15 in the second. Longer observation cannot distinguish them. By Equation~\eqref{eq:event-law}, each finite commit sequence has the same probability under both models.

These models are examples of hidden Markov models with a binary activity indicator. Here the probability of activity in the next minute is $\pi$ whatever the engineer did in the current minute. This independence permits the two parameter pairs above. For hidden Markov models with finitely many possible hidden values, generic identification results allow exceptional parameter values and interchangeable labels \cite[Section~6.1]{allman2009}.\footnote{In a hidden Markov model, the current hidden value determines the distribution of the next one. Conditional on the entire hidden sequence, each observation depends only on the corresponding hidden value, and the observations are independent. Generic identification means that, outside an exceptional set, the distribution of observations determines the parameters up to a permutation of the labels and their associated parameters.} An additional restriction on how activity persists from one minute to the next might exclude one of the parameter pairs. Measurements of activity or features of code changes could distinguish the models if they have different distributions under the two pairs.

Even if commit times identify the model parameters, interpreting $Z_t=1$ as a minute of human work requires independent evidence about the engineer's time. An agent, for example, can produce a commit while the engineer works on a different task. Observing the engineer's time directly puts limits on the labour that could fall inside a given period. To use those limits, we must first define the task and decide when to count work towards it.

\section{Task accounting with explicit boundaries}
\label{sec:accounting}

\subsection{Defining tasks and allocating human time}

Task $i$ enters the study at time $s_i$, when its description gives the requested behaviour and acceptance criteria and the task meets the inclusion rules. Both the labour total and the time to acceptance start at $s_i$. Engineers may already have spent hours eliciting the request and drafting those criteria. To measure the labour spent on all incoming requests, follow each from arrival, including those that never reach intake. For the fixed workload studied here, follow each requirement from intake as its commits and pull requests change. Keep each version of the requirement and decide in advance how changes of scope, splits and mergers affect its score.

When a task splits, keep its identifier and link each component to the parent. Decide before observing outcomes whether to credit components individually or only when the parent is complete. Count each contribution once. If one component meets the deadline and another misses it, the first contributes to a component-based score. Under the parent-only rule, the parent scores zero because one component missed the deadline. The two totals can therefore differ even when the component scores sum to the parent's score.

For each group, list the AI tools and model versions available for each class of task. Specify which actions a tool may take autonomously and which require a named reviewer or release check. Track actual use separately. Engineers may turn to a tool for a difficult task or choose to work without it. A comparison based on observed use can therefore mix the effect of access with differences in task difficulty.

How should human time be counted when several AI workflows run at once? Count the time each person spends on the work and calculate automated execution time separately. Let $a_{ijr}(t)$ be the fraction of person $j$'s time spent on task $i$ doing work of type $r$, such as implementation or review, at calendar time $t$. Each person's combined allocation is at most one. The integral of this fraction over time measures the person's hours on that activity. The task total sums these hours across contributors and types of work. With finite sets of tasks, people and types of work, and measurable allocation functions,\footnote{The integral $\int_s^e a(t)\,dt$ accumulates the allocation fraction over elapsed time. A constant allocation $a$ over $e-s$ hours contributes $a(e-s)$ person-hours. These are Lebesgue integrals with respect to time. Measurability ensures that sets defined by allocation thresholds have a well-defined duration. The phrase ``almost everywhere'' used below permits exceptions on a set of total duration zero, such as individual switching instants. Such exceptions leave these integrals unchanged.} labour through task age $u\geq0$, measured from the start of tracking, is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:labour}
 a_{ijr}(t)\geq0,\qquad \sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1,\qquad
 H_i(u)=\sum_{j,r}\int_{s_i}^{s_i+u}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt.
\end{equation}
With time in hours, $H_i(u)$ has units of person-hours. Two people working for an hour can therefore contribute two person-hours. The allocation constraint limits each person's combined contribution during that hour, including when several sessions run concurrently.

Two workflows can continue running while the engineer moves from prompting one to reviewing the other's result. Supervision takes human time and counts towards the task being supervised. A minute spent reviewing one result uses a minute of the engineer's time, even if both workflows run throughout it.

For example, consider one person using two AI workflows during a one-hour interval. The person prepares workflow A's prompt during minutes 0--10 and submits it at minute 10. Workflow A starts then and finishes at minute 60. The person prepares and submits workflow B's prompt during minutes 10--20. Workflow B executes from minute 20 to minute 40, after which the person reviews its result until minute 60. Assume no other human work on these tasks during the interval. Human labour totals forty minutes, or $2/3$ of a person-hour. The two workflows ran for a combined seventy minutes, or $7/6$ workflow-hours. Figure~\ref{fig:human-and-workflow-time} shows how both totals arise.

Within the post-intake window, count time spent clarifying the requirement, implementing it and checking or repairing the result. Specify when waiting for a workflow demands the engineer's attention and counts as labour. For shared review or platform work, specify how to divide the hours across the tasks it supports in Equation~\eqref{eq:labour}. If the available evidence leaves the task unknown, keep those hours visible as work with uncertain attribution.

One workflow-hour means one hour of elapsed execution for one workflow. Define the events that start and stop the execution clock, then add the time between those events for each run inside the observation period. Two concurrent runs contribute both durations, including during human supervision. CPU time, token counts and charges have their own units. To combine them with human labour in a cost estimate, apply an explicit price to each resource used.

Let $T_i$ be the elapsed time from $s_i$ to first acceptance. Between intake and acceptance, this clock runs while engineers think through a solution, a task waits in a queue or a workflow executes unattended. For permanent abandonment before acceptance, set $T_i=\infty$. If observation ends while acceptance is still possible, the last observation time marks the end of follow-up and the later outcome is unknown. Keep the first acceptance timestamp distinct from the deployment timestamp when the two events occur at different times.

\subsection{Scoring tasks and checking retained output}

Let $S_i^0\geq0$ denote the reference score for task $i$. For a comparison of fixed requirements, score the description available at intake. A score of one weights each task equally. If the score counts functional units, a requirement containing more units carries greater weight. Standard Coding Hours can also supply weights if readers can identify their units and the inputs used to calculate them. Use one version of the scoring rule across groups and keep the inputs used for each task. Its initial weight then stays fixed even if the team delivers the requirement in fewer hours.

A score calculated from final code can change with the implementation. AI access could then affect both the hours spent on a task and the weight the task carries in the output total. Report this dependence if the comparison uses final code. Even with identical final behaviour, splitting or squashing changes can alter a nonlinear score. To avoid counting the same requirement twice, score its original description once or use a rule under which component scores sum to the whole. Credit the delivered requirement once across all attempts, including retries and reverts.

The reference score determines the weight of a requirement in the output total. A labour forecast predicts the person-hours the task will take through a specified age. Using a prediction expressed in Standard Coding Hours as a forecast of person-hours requires comparison with independently measured hours for the same work over the same period. Section~\ref{sec:labour-prediction-validation} describes a reference forecast based on the hours spent on earlier tasks.

Set the acceptance deadline at task age $\tau>0$. For tasks accepted by that deadline, check the requirement $\Delta\geq0$ time units after first acceptance. Let $R_i(\Delta)\in[0,1]$ denote the fraction of the original requirement still satisfied at calendar time $s_i+T_i+\Delta$, judged against criteria specified before follow-up. The retained reference score is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
 Q_i(\tau,\Delta)=\begin{cases} S_i^0 R_i(\Delta),&T_i\leq\tau,\\ 0,&T_i>\tau.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Suppose the team accepts a ten-unit requirement before the deadline. If 0.8 of the requirement remains satisfied at follow-up, the task contributes eight reference units. Reviewers need criteria for scoring partial fulfilment and for distinguishing deliberate replacement of a requirement from a defect. Counting surviving lines alone cannot establish which behaviour the software retains. Passing tests cover the cases exercised, and incident reports depend on someone detecting and reporting a failure. Identify which defects the checks can reveal and measure how often reviewers agree.

For a finite cohort with positive total labour, productivity is its total retained score divided by all hours attributed to that cohort through the common task age $\tau+\Delta$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cohort-productivity}
 P_H(\tau,\Delta)
 =\frac{\sum_i Q_i(\tau,\Delta)}{\sum_i H_i(\tau+\Delta)}.
\end{equation}
A task that misses the deadline contributes zero to the numerator. Its hours still enter the denominator. A task completed entirely by automation can contribute to the numerator even when its human labour is zero. If the cohort uses zero person-hours, report the two totals and leave productivity undefined. When either total is estimated, the ratio also needs an uncertainty calculation (Section~\ref{sec:difference-contract}).

For each task, the denominator counts work from intake through age $\tau+\Delta$. Those dates fix the observation window. The hours people worked within it may still be unknown. A total is \emph{point-identified} for a realised history when at least one allocation fits the observations and the total is the same for every such allocation. Proposition~\ref{prop:nonidentification} concerns a population parameter governing the distribution of commit sequences. Here we seek the smallest and largest labour totals compatible with one history.

\section{Estimating labour from sampled allocations}
\label{sec:estimation}

Suppose a team samples engineers' time to estimate total labour. A different draw from the same workforce can yield a different estimate. Treating either estimate as an exact total in the linear programme could exclude the hours people actually worked. To estimate those hours, weight the sampled intervals by the inverse of their selection probabilities. The sampling design also determines how the estimate varies across possible draws.

To draw the sample, list every interval in which each contributor could have worked on the requirements. This list, the sampling frame, must cover time spent thinking through a design, reviewing a change or helping a colleague, even when nobody edits code. For each selected interval, a short diary entry or a dated task note can identify how much time the contributor spent on the work. Have an independent reviewer check a subsample and explain any differences, naming the evidence that supports each correction. Missed prompts and errors of recall affect what we learn about intervals on the list. An omitted shift has no chance of selection and requires a check against a separate source, such as the on-call schedule.

\section{Validating the measurements}
\label{sec:study}

The proposed pilot first checks whose hours the sampling frame covers. Define which work belongs to the cohort and how time spent across several tasks will count. Before collection begins, fix each requirement's acceptance deadline and the criteria for judging how much of it the software satisfies at follow-up.

Start with the people who can contribute to an enrolled requirement. A reviewer may discuss a release or an on-call engineer may investigate an incident without appearing among the commit authors. Include their eligible hours, even on shifts with no repository activity. Some contributors will remember the work but be unable to name the ticket. Their time still counts towards the cohort total if every plausible task belongs to the cohort and the work falls within each task's labour window. If any plausible task falls outside that window or the cohort, allow for both possibilities when calculating the bounds.

Ask about a randomly selected, completed interval. Allow contributors to report zero minutes, divide time between tasks or describe work whose task they cannot identify. Keep unanswered prompts in the sample. Summarise response rates and time spent reporting by role and study group, and by activity where independently known. For intervals that remain unresolved, bound the missing hours by their durations and any independent evidence. Carry those ranges through the estimate and its uncertainty calculation. Report whether any admissible values move the result across the decision threshold.

Select audit shifts independently of the replies. A reviewer can use contemporaneous evidence to check a contributor's account. Agreement can conceal a shared omission, such as a design conversation absent from both accounts. To assess accuracy, compare the reported hours with a separate source that covers the work in question, such as independently timed reviews with confirmed attendance. Justify an error limit for the work this source covers. Bound the hours both accounts could have missed elsewhere by the remaining eligible time and any further evidence. Report disagreement and collection time separately.

To measure how much a person's shared time limit narrows a labour range, fix the target window and the error limits for each observation. Calculate the range twice, keeping the same contributors, observations and target in both programmes. In the first, give each of a person's streams the full cell duration as its upper limit. In the second, also require their combined hours to fit within the cell. The difference in widths, measured in person-hours, isolates the effect of this additional constraint. Adding separately calculated ranges would also discard dependencies induced by observations. If two people on different tasks report one hour in total, each task permits zero to one hour. The observation fixes their combined labour at one hour.

To judge whether predictions improve an estimate of hours already worked, compare its sampling variance with the variance obtained under the same design without predictions. Keep the probability that each relevant pair of intervals is selected together whenever the variance formula uses it. Issue a planning forecast at intake, before work in the forecast window begins. Check it against subsequent measurements for the same window. If those hours are estimated from a sample, the apparent forecast error includes sampling error.

\subsection{Conditions for comparing AI tools and working practices}

AI access can affect which requests reach a team and which tasks it takes on. A study of a fixed workload therefore needs eligibility rules set before any task, person or team enters a comparison group. Specify how to count work already in progress and keep each request's disposition, including postponement, rejection or abandonment. To measure the effect on incoming organisational work, follow requests and work in progress over the same calendar period in both groups. In either design, identify the population to which the result applies and explain how the analysis weights the observations to represent it.

A reviewer who works with two teams can carry a technique learned with AI into the comparison team. Reusable components can spread benefits in the same way. Teams that compete for computing time can also delay one another. Identify these links before forming the groups and allow for them in the analysis. Compare teams in the groups they originally entered. Measure actual tool use separately so that switching tools does not move a team between groups after the study begins.

Calculate uncertainty at the level at which the study allocates AI access. If it randomises teams, a thousand commits from one team still contribute only one team to the comparison. With randomisation within pairs, use the pairs to calculate uncertainty. The uncertainty calculation must also allow for sampling contributors' hours and auditing quality on a subset of tasks.

Use the same scoring criteria and follow-up procedure in both groups. Have two reviewers assess a subset of tasks independently to measure disagreement. Note when feedback reaches the team, since a review can prompt repair, and disclose any case in which a reviewer learns which group produced the work. Count resulting repairs within the labour window for the productivity ratio. To estimate the score for the whole cohort from the audited subset, use the tasks' known selection probabilities and specify how feedback could affect subsequent work. List severe defects separately so that a high retained score cannot conceal a reliability or security failure.

Follow every task through age $\tau+\Delta$ (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}). Distinguish a task the team permanently abandons from one whose later progress the study can no longer observe. For missing outcomes, specify the assumptions used to estimate them or the range they could occupy. Abandoning more tasks could lower the hours worked. Report those hours alongside acceptance and the score at follow-up so readers can judge the apparent saving. When total labour is positive, accompany the productivity ratio with both totals and account for their uncertainty, including their covariance where relevant.

Before observing outcomes, choose the smallest labour saving that would justify changing practice. Fix the largest acceptable loss of retained output, the noninferiority margin. Even a small estimated loss leaves this criterion unresolved if the uncertainty interval extends beyond the margin.

\section{What a field study must establish}
\label{sec:limits}

The engineer in the opening example worked three hours in a four-hour period. Learning that a two-hour review occupied the entire second half fixes the first half at exactly one hour. In the constructed histories, a comparable observation spanning both tasks fixed first-hour labour in all 96 cases. The shared time limit helped when the timing of those activities was unknown. Once a reviewer disappeared from the observations, even a narrow range could exclude the work the two people actually did.

A field pilot should first establish who contributed and what evidence covers their time, including review and incident work away from the repository. On independently selected shifts, compare contributors' accounts with a reference collected separately and bound hours both could miss. Measure reporting time as well. The pilot can then show what error limits the evidence supports and what it costs to obtain them. Forecasts issued at intake can then be assessed against later labour on the same tasks, with the reference uncertainty carried into the comparison. For a trial of AI access, compare plausible differences in reporting error between groups with the labour saving that would justify a change in practice. If that error could account for the saving, better measurement is needed before recruiting more teams.
