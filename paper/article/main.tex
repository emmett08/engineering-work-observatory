\begin{abstract}
Using artificial intelligence (AI) can change how events recorded by software development tools relate to human effort. Labour measures that effort in person-hours. To relate effort to delivery, we also define time to acceptance and an output score at follow-up. For each task, this score equals its initial reference score multiplied by the fraction of the accepted requirement still satisfied. Tasks missing the acceptance deadline receive an output score of zero. We examine what interval observations establish about labour and how sampled labour can correct a telemetry prediction of it.

For observed interval totals or justified bounds on them, each person's available time constrains the labour possible within a target window. Splitting time at the boundaries of observation intervals and target windows gives a finite linear programme with exactly the same possible labour totals, including when observations overlap or tasks share a person's time. Accounting jointly for shared capacity can rule out totals included when separately calculated interval bounds are added. Whenever the observations admit an allocation, the minimum, maximum and every total between them are attainable. Each total can also be realised with one activity at a time per person, provided activities may start and stop at arbitrary times.

For probability-sampled labour, we correct a telemetry prediction total with observed-minus-predicted differences weighted by inverse inclusion probabilities. Predictions are calculated before sampling and kept unchanged across possible samples. With known positive inclusion probabilities and accurately measured sampled labour, the corrected estimate averages to true labour over repeated samples. Its variance can be higher or lower than that of estimation without predictions. Analytical proofs, Lean-checked interval-integral and schedule-construction lemmas, and exact finite calculations establish the results within these allocation and sampling models. Empirical validation of the proposed measurements is the next step towards using them to compare AI-assisted work.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the observations determine}
\label{sec:introduction}

Suppose a team records three person-hours of work during a four-hour interval. How much work occurred during its first two hours? If one person did all the work, that person can supply at most two person-hours in either half. The first half must therefore contain between one and two person-hours. The person could work one hour in the first half and two in the second, or two in the first and one in the second, so both totals are possible. The interval total thus determines a range of first-half labour totals. Commit times can leave a further ambiguity, since the same event sequence can arise from different amounts of human activity.

\emph{Labour} is human time allocated to the included work, measured in person-hours. For software work assisted by artificial intelligence (AI), this includes unsuccessful work and post-acceptance repair as well as the work leading to acceptance. To interpret this effort alongside what the work delivers, we also define elapsed time to first acceptance and retained output. Retained output equals the task's initial reference score multiplied by the fraction of its accepted requirement still satisfied at follow-up, with zero credit for tasks missing the acceptance deadline.

When tasks share a person's time, their allocations across tasks and types of work must sum to at most one. This is the capacity constraint used in the opening example. When this constraint and the observations permit at least one allocation, our linear programme gives the minimum and maximum consistent labour totals. Every total between that minimum and maximum is possible under the same constraints. A higher minimum or lower maximum therefore needs additional observations or justified restrictions on how people allocated their time.

When exact labour totals are unavailable, a probability sample of person-intervals (time intervals for individual people) can support estimation. Predictions calculated before sampling supply an initial total, corrected by the sampled differences between observed and predicted labour, each weighted by inverse inclusion probability. With known positive inclusion probabilities and accurately measured allocations, this estimator is unbiased over repeated samples. The correction improves precision when its sampling variance is lower than that of the labour estimate without predictions. Telemetry can therefore help estimate labour even when commit events leave its exact value unresolved.

These two uses of observations provide different guarantees. An identification interval contains exactly the labour totals consistent with specified observations and allocation constraints. Sampling uncertainty describes how an estimate varies with the selected sample. Both concern the measurement of labour. Estimating how AI changes labour or output additionally requires a causal study design.

Standard Coder illustrates the distinction between telemetry-derived activity and measured labour. Wright and Ziegler's approach \cite{wright2019} infers coding activity from commit timing and predicts the resulting labels from code-change features. Standard Coding Hours can serve as a common reference index, with calibration against independently observed labour required for interpreting it as person-hours. AI may alter both how human activity produces commits and how much work a code change represents. Forsgren et al.\ place activity and efficiency within a broader account of developer productivity \cite{forsgren2021}, while Mozannar et al.\ examine the interaction time costs of AI-assisted programming \cite{mozannar2024}.

Existing AI studies measure different outcomes in different populations. Peng et al.\ examine completion time among completers of a programming exercise \cite{peng2023}. Becker et al.\ study experienced maintainers in familiar repositories, comparing log completion times after adjustment for forecast times \cite{becker2025}. Cui et al.\ study weekly completed pull requests in organisational experiments \cite{cui2026}, using allocation to study groups as an instrument for adoption in their June 2025 manuscript \cite[Section 4.1 and Table 3]{cui2025manuscript}. The studies differ in their populations, AI tools, working practices and measured outcomes. Their estimates therefore answer different questions. A subsequent report from Model Evaluation and Threat Research describes selection and concurrent-agent measurement difficulties, further motivating separate labour and elapsed-time records \cite{metr2026}. The calculations here use no data from these studies.

Sections~\ref{sec:observation} and~\ref{sec:accounting} explain why commits alone need not determine person-hours and define labour, acceptance time and retained output. Section~\ref{sec:sharp-identification} derives the minimum and maximum possible labour totals, and Section~\ref{sec:estimation} uses sampled labour to correct fixed telemetry predictions. Section~\ref{sec:study} sets out how to validate these measurements before comparing AI tools and working practices. The proofs apply standard optimisation and sampling results to the model's constraints on allocating people's time. Appendix~\ref{app:scalar} gives a shortest-path solution for one person's work of one type on one task.

\section{Why event telemetry alone need not identify labour}
\label{sec:observation}

To examine what commits reveal about human activity, let a minute-indexed hidden variable $Z_t\in\{0,1\}$ indicate an activity state, and let $C_t\in\{0,1\}$ record a commit event. A fitted event model specifies how activity changes from minute to minute and how commits depend on that activity. Interpreting $Z_t=1$ as ``coding'' requires validation against independently measured labour. Within the model, different activity distributions can produce the same commit distribution, as the following construction shows.

\begin{proposition}[Failure of unrestricted identification]
\label{prop:nonidentification}
Suppose the states $Z_t$ are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter $\pi\in(0,1)$, and, independently across minutes and of the states, $B_t$ are Bernoulli variables with parameter $c\in(0,1)$. Define $C_t=Z_tB_t$. The joint distribution of all commit observations depends on $(\pi,c)$ only through $\pi c$. In particular, $(\pi,c)=(1/2,1/5)$ and $(1/4,2/5)$ give identical commit distributions but different expected active times.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each $C_t$ is Bernoulli with parameter $\theta=\pi c$, and the $C_t$ are independent. For every $n\geq1$ and every binary string $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:event-law}
 \Prb(C_1=e_1,\ldots,C_n=e_n)
 =\theta^{\sum_t e_t}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum_t e_t}.
\end{equation}
Both parameter pairs give $\theta=1/10$. In contrast,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:activity-total}
 \E\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_t\right]=n\pi,
\end{equation}
which is $n/2$ minutes in the first model and $n/4$ in the second. Thus identical commit distributions can correspond to different expected numbers of active minutes.
\end{proof}

This construction belongs to the independent-state subfamily of a two-state hidden Markov model, in which the next activity state has the same distribution for either current state. It establishes an ambiguity in the unrestricted model family. Generic-identifiability results for finite-state hidden Markov models concern parameters outside exceptional sets and permit hidden-state relabelling \cite[Section~6.1]{allman2009}. Identifying the parameters therefore requires an explicit account of the restrictions or observations that distinguish the candidate models. Restrictions on the activity model, informative code-change features or independent activity observations may supply that distinction.

Identifying the activity model leaves a further measurement question. Interpreting its activity states as human labour requires validation against independently measured person-hours. Under AI assistance, commits can continue while human attention is elsewhere, so the mechanism that produces commits may itself change. Measured interval totals constrain how much labour occurred during a specified period. To use those measurements, we first define which work belongs to each task and the periods over which it is counted.

\section{Task accounting with explicit boundaries}
\label{sec:accounting}

\subsection{Defining tasks and allocating human time}

To count a task consistently as its commits and pull requests change, use its recorded requirement as the reference. Begin tracking task $i$ at time $s_i$, when its requested behaviour, conditions for inclusion in the study and acceptance criteria are recorded. Task splits, mergers and scope changes can then alter what completion requires. Their treatment in the records and analysis must be defined before observing outcomes, with the history of changes to requirements and acceptance criteria retained for that analysis.

A split task can retain its identifier while separate records describe its components. Specify whether accepted components count separately or through the completed parent, with each contribution counted once. This choice affects when output is recognised. Suppose one component is accepted before a deadline and another afterwards. Counting the components recognises the first by the deadline, while counting their parent waits for both. Regrouping can therefore change counted output even when the component reference scores, defined in the next subsection, add to the parent's score.

AI access defines part of the working conditions being compared. Its specification must identify the available tools and model versions, together with the tasks and autonomous actions permitted. Named reviewers and release requirements describe the human oversight governing the resulting work. These records describe what each group is allowed to do. Actual use needs a separate record alongside access and working practices, because difficult tasks may prompt more tool use while people may decline an available tool. Comparing groups by use alone can therefore mix the effect of access with differences in task difficulty.

The next accounting question is how much human time each task receives. Let $a_{ijr}(t)$ be the fraction of person $j$'s time spent on task $i$ doing work of type $r$, such as implementation or review, at calendar time $t$. Each person's allocations across tasks and types of work sum to at most one. Integrating these fractions over time and summing across people and types of work gives cumulative task labour. With finite sets of tasks, people and types of work, and measurable allocation functions, labour through task age $u\geq0$, measured from the start of tracking, is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:labour}
 a_{ijr}(t)\geq0,\qquad \sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1,\qquad
 H_i(u)=\sum_{j,r}\int_{s_i}^{s_i+u}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt.
\end{equation}
With time in hours, $H_i(u)$ has units of person-hours. Summing across people allows pair work to contribute two person-hours in one elapsed hour. The allocation constraint limits each person's combined contribution during that hour, including when several sessions run concurrently.

Several AI-assisted workflows can execute concurrently for the same person. The allocation functions record the human time spent prompting, supervising, reviewing and integrating their results. A person can switch between these activities while the workflows continue running. Attributing fractions of the person's time across tasks preserves the shared capacity constraint and prevents the same human time being counted in full against several workflows.

For a constructed example, suppose two workflows run throughout an hour. Their operator spends ten minutes prompting one and twenty minutes reviewing the other in separate periods, with no other human time devoted to the included work during that hour. The labour total is half a person-hour. Record one hour of execution for each workflow in the computing-resource account described below.

Applying this account requires deciding which activities and shared contributions belong to each task. Include all work from specification and implementation through review, testing and repair. The inclusion rule must state whether attended waiting belongs in the total. Shared review and platform work require a rule for attributing person-hours to the tasks in Equation~\eqref{eq:labour}. Work that the observations leave unattributed needs a separate record.

Unattended tool execution belongs in the computing-resource account. Reporting agent running time requires an explicit definition of an agent instance and the resource categories being counted. Report that time alongside token use and charges, keeping their units separate from person-hours. Combining these quantities requires an explicit economic conversion and changes the question from labour to cost.

Elapsed time to acceptance records how long a task takes, including periods within its tracking window when it is queued or running unattended. Let $T_i$ be elapsed time from the start of tracking to first acceptance. Starting this clock at $s_i$ includes queueing and any reasoning and specification between $s_i$ and the first commit. If the task is permanently abandoned before acceptance, put $T_i=\infty$. This records abandonment as an outcome, while loss of observation is censoring. Acceptance and deployment may occur at different times and then require separate records.

\subsection{Scoring tasks and checking retained output}

Comparing output requires a common reference for what each task delivers. Assign each task a nonnegative reference score $S_i^0$, recording the scoring-rule version and the task information it uses. The score may count tasks or functional units, or use Standard Coding Hours as weights. Retained output has the same unit as this score, and productivity is retained output per person-hour. The superscript $0$ denotes the common reference. Keep that reference consistent by retaining each task's score and using the same scoring rule and weights across comparison groups.

The choice of scoring input determines whether that reference depends on the AI tool being compared. For a causal comparison of fixed task requirements, prefer the requirements recorded when tracking begins. AI can change the final code, so a retrospective code score depends on the resulting implementation. Such a score can still be reported with this dependence made explicit. A nonlinear scoring rule also allows splitting or squashing a change to alter its score. To keep the reference consistent, score the same recorded task information once, or use a requirement-scoring rule whose component scores add to its total. Count the delivered requirement once across failed attempts, retries and reverts.

A score expressed in person-hours needs calibration against independently measured labour. That calibration must identify whose labour is included and the types of work counted, together with the quality requirements and the event at which labour recording ends. Training a predictor on imputed activity states teaches it to predict those states. Interpreting its predictions as person-hours requires independent validation against labour measured on the specified basis.

For each task accepted by the deadline, multiply its initial reference score by the fraction of its accepted requirement still satisfied at follow-up. This product is its retained output score. A task missing the deadline contributes an output score of zero. Choose an acceptance deadline at task age $\tau>0$ and a follow-up duration $\Delta\geq0$ beginning at first acceptance. Specify the criteria for judging what fraction of an accepted requirement remains satisfied. For accepted tasks, let $R_i(\Delta)\in[0,1]$ be that fraction at time $s_i+T_i+\Delta$, judged using these criteria. Retained reference output is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
 Q_i(\tau,\Delta)=\begin{cases} S_i^0 R_i(\Delta),&T_i\leq\tau,\\ 0,&T_i>\tau.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
As a constructed example, suppose a task has an initial reference score of ten units and is accepted before the deadline. A retained fraction of 0.8 yields an output score of eight reference units. Calculating that fraction requires a rule for assessing partial requirement satisfaction and distinguishing intentional replacement from defect-driven loss. Surviving lines, successful tests and absence of recorded incidents each provide incomplete evidence of requirement satisfaction. The follow-up procedure must document which defects its checks can detect and measure agreement between reviewers.

The labour and output accounts now give a productivity ratio. For a specified finite set of tasks with positive total labour, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cohort-productivity}
 P_H(\tau,\Delta)
 =\frac{\sum_i Q_i(\tau,\Delta)}{\sum_i H_i(\tau+\Delta)}.
\end{equation}
The denominator counts labour through the common task age $\tau+\Delta$ for every task in that set. It therefore includes labour on rejected, unfinished and abandoned tasks, together with attributable work after acceptance. Within this common window, early acceptance leaves more time for repair than late acceptance. Ending repair accounting exactly $\Delta$ after each acceptance would instead make the end of labour recording depend on acceptance time and define a different denominator. Measuring the ratio defined here requires recording when labour observation ends and following every task for $\tau+\Delta$.

An individual task with zero human labour can still contribute retained output. The positivity requirement applies to the tasks' combined labour and determines when the productivity ratio is defined. Report retained output and total person-hours separately so the ratio can be interpreted alongside its numerator and denominator.

\section{Estimating labour from sampled allocations}
\label{sec:estimation}

The preceding bounds describe every labour total permitted by given interval observations. Sampling raises the question of how selected person-intervals can support an estimate of total labour. The estimate varies with the selected intervals, so entering it as an exact observation can exclude the true allocation through sampling error. We therefore develop its sampling properties separately. The difference estimator below uses sampled labour to correct a telemetry prediction total. Under its sampling and measurement assumptions, the corrected estimate averages to the true labour total over repeated samples.

The sampling frame determines which work the estimate can represent. This complete list of person-intervals must cover all target work, including off-keyboard reasoning, review and coordination. Short diaries and task records can show how much of each selected interval a person spent on that work. Assess recording accuracy through independent checks of a subsample. Record the reviewers, evidence examined and resolution of each discrepancy. Report disagreement and investigate its causes. Nonresponse and inaccurate recall concern the records within the frame, while omitted work concerns the frame's coverage. Each requires its own investigation.

\section{Validating the measurements}
\label{sec:study}

The mathematical results depend on observations that cover the target work and measure its person-hours accurately. A measurement pilot must test these premises before comparing AI tools. Define the included tasks and types of work, how hours are attributed to them and which person-intervals are available for sampling. Record the reference scoring rule and acceptance deadline together with the retained-output criteria before collection. These choices determine what the measurements describe.

The pilot must establish how reliably and at what effort those records can be collected. Include periods with concurrent AI workflows when checking attribution of shared oversight and attended waiting against the inclusion rules. Record disagreement over allocations and nonresponse, and track follow-up completeness and collection time. Report each by comparison group, type of work and explicitly defined task category. This evidence informs the sampling effort and precision that a later comparison can support.

Validation must also address the particular guarantee being used. Interval bounds require independently checked totals with justified limits on measurement error. For target windows chosen before observing outcomes, compare the sums of separately calculated lower and upper labour bounds with the joint minimum and maximum. This shows whether accounting jointly for each person's available time raises the minimum or lowers the maximum.

For sampled labour, the question is whether fixed predictions improve precision. Compare the variance over repeated samples with and without predictions calculated before sampling and kept unchanged whichever sample is drawn. Retain the inclusion probabilities and joint sampling-design information needed to calculate uncertainty. \subsection{Conditions for comparing AI tools and working practices}

Accurate labour and output measurements support a comparison between groups. Attributing a difference to AI access also requires a causal design that defines which work its conclusion concerns. AI adoption can change which work is requested or undertaken, so comparing a fixed workload and studying an organisation's incoming work answer different questions. For a fixed-workload comparison, set the conditions for including tasks before allocating tasks, people or teams to comparison groups, and keep those conditions unchanged. State how work done before group allocation is counted, and retain records of postponed, rejected and abandoned requests. An organisation-wide adoption comparison needs a common calendar window and records of incoming requests and work in progress. Each design must identify its target population and explain how observations are weighted to represent it.

Group formation must account for work shared between teams. Reviewers, limited computing resources for agents and reusable components can transmit the effects of one team's AI access to another. The group boundaries and outcome analysis must therefore account for these routes of influence. Analyse outcomes by the original group allocation, recording actual tool use separately.

Whether teams, people or other units are allocated to groups determines the level at which causal uncertainty must be calculated. More commits within a team add records while leaving the number of independently allocated teams unchanged. The uncertainty calculation must also account for sampling labour and checking quality in subsets.

Retained-output comparisons require common criteria and a common follow-up procedure across groups. Two reviewers should check a subset independently so their disagreement can be measured. The check can itself trigger repair, making the timing of feedback and any loss of reviewer blinding---reviewers learning which group a task belongs to---part of the measurement record. Count resulting repair within the labour window used for the productivity denominator. Extending retained-output estimates from a checked subset to all tasks requires known selection probabilities and assumptions addressing any effect of the check on subsequent work. Report severe defects separately because a retained fraction cannot describe every security or reliability consequence.

Interpreting an apparent labour saving requires the corresponding acceptance and retained-output records. Complete follow-up through $\tau+\Delta$ as required by Section~\ref{sec:accounting}, distinguishing permanent abandonment from loss of observation. Incomplete follow-up requires explicit assumptions or bounds for missing outcomes. Report labour, acceptance and retained output together, since reduced labour may accompany more abandonment. Present retained output and positive total labour alongside their productivity ratio, accounting for uncertainty in both quantities and their dependence.

The decision criterion must then specify how much improvement is useful and how much quality loss is acceptable. Prespecify the improvement thresholds and the maximum acceptable reduction in quality (the noninferiority margin). Establishing that a loss lies within this margin requires evidence about its magnitude and uncertainty. Failure to detect a reduction alone leaves that requirement unresolved. \section{Scope and next observations}
\label{sec:limits}

Different distributions of human activity can produce identical commit distributions. Interval totals or justified bounds, together with each person's available time, restrict how much work could have occurred during a specified period. Splitting time at the boundaries of every observation interval and target window gives a finite optimisation problem with exactly the same possible labour totals. This joint formulation enforces the shared capacity that links a person's tasks. Adding separately calculated interval bounds can include totals inconsistent with that capacity. The summed lower and upper bounds are exact only if work allocations achieving the respective component bounds can occur together.

Probability sampling permits design-unbiased estimation when the frame covers the target work, inclusion probabilities are known and positive, and sampled allocations are measured accurately. A fixed telemetry predictor improves precision only when estimating its total residual has lower sampling variance than estimating labour without predictions. Applying these guarantees requires evidence that the records measure the intended work. Diaries and task records can supply the observations, but whether they capture reasoning and coordination and accurately record how people divide their time requires empirical checking.

Establishing a causal benefit from AI adoption requires a comparison of AI tools and working practices. An organisation-wide adoption study would include changes to incoming requirements, whereas the proposed experiment concerns a fixed workload. The next empirical step towards that experiment is the measurement pilot specified in Section~\ref{sec:causal-experiment}. It must test whether task records capture person-hours across the included types of work and support independent checks of retained output through follow-up. Disagreement, missing records and collection effort will inform the measurement procedure and size of the proposed fixed-workload experiment.
