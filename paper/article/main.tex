\begin{abstract}
Software-work telemetry records events whose relationship to human time can change under AI assistance. We determine which labour totals, measured in allocated person-hours, are consistent with specified observations. We report elapsed time to acceptance and output retained at follow-up separately. Observed interval totals or justified lower and upper bounds, together with each person's available time, constrain how much labour could have occurred within a target window. Splitting time at every observation endpoint and target-window boundary gives a finite linear programme with the same possible labour totals, even when observations overlap or tasks share a person's time. Minimising and maximising the target total gives its exact bounds; every total between them is possible under the same constraints, even when each person does one activity at a time and may switch activities at arbitrary times. Adding bounds calculated separately for each interval can include totals that no joint allocation can produce. For a probability sample, we add differences between observed and predicted labour, weighted by inverse inclusion probabilities, to the total of predictions fixed before sampling. Under the stated sampling and measurement assumptions, this estimate averages to true labour over repeated samples. Its variance can be higher or lower than that of the estimator without predictions. We supply analytical proofs and exact finite computational checks using constructed data. The proposed measurements still require empirical validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the observations determine}
\label{sec:introduction}

Suppose a team records three person-hours of work during a four-hour interval. How much work occurred during its first two hours? If one person did all the work, at least one person-hour must fall in the first half, because at most two can fall in the second. At most two person-hours can fall in the first half. The person could work one hour in the first half and two in the second, or two in the first and one in the second. The recorded total therefore does not tell us exactly how much work occurred in the first two hours. Commit times alone may not even determine total labour: the same event sequence can arise from different amounts of human activity.

\emph{Labour} is human time allocated to the included work, measured in person-hours. For AI-assisted software work, we count the time spent on all included types of work and report elapsed time to first acceptance and reference output retained at follow-up separately. The accounting includes unsuccessful work and post-acceptance repair. At any time, the fractions of a person's time allocated to different tasks and types of work must sum to at most one. Our linear programme gives the minimum and maximum labour totals consistent with the observed interval totals or justified bounds and each person's available time. Every total between those endpoints is possible under the same constraints. Establishing a higher minimum or a lower maximum therefore requires additional observations or justified restrictions on how people allocated their time.

When exact labour totals are unavailable, a probability sample of person-intervals (time intervals for individual people) can give an estimate that is unbiased over repeated samples, provided inclusion probabilities are known and positive and allocations are measured accurately. For each sampled person-interval, divide observed labour minus predicted labour by the interval's inclusion probability. Add these weighted differences to the total of predictions fixed before sampling. This estimator improves precision when the sum of weighted differences has lower variance over repeated samples than the estimator without predictions. Telemetry can therefore improve a labour estimate even when commit events do not uniquely determine labour. Sampling uncertainty describes how an estimate varies with the selected sample. An identification interval instead contains exactly the labour totals consistent with the specified observations and allocation constraints. Estimating how AI changes labour or output additionally requires a causal study design.

Wright and Ziegler's Standard Coder approach \cite{wright2019} infers coding activity from commit timing and predicts the resulting labels from code-change features. Standard Coding Hours (SCH) can serve as a common reference index. Using SCH to measure person-hours requires calibration against independently observed labour. AI may alter both how human activity produces commits and how much work a code change represents. The SPACE framework places activity and efficiency within a broader account of developer productivity \cite{forsgren2021}. Mozannar et al.\ examine interaction time costs in AI-assisted programming \cite{mozannar2024}.

Existing AI studies measure different outcomes in different populations. Peng et al.\ examine completion time among completers of a programming exercise \cite{peng2023}. Becker et al.\ study experienced maintainers in familiar repositories, comparing log completion times after adjustment for forecast times \cite{becker2025}. Cui et al.\ study weekly completed pull requests in organisational experiments \cite{cui2026}, using allocation to study groups as an instrument for adoption in their June 2025 manuscript \cite[Section 4.1 and Table 3]{cui2025manuscript}. Their estimates concern different populations, AI tools and working practices, and outcomes, so they cannot be substituted for one another as estimates of the same quantity. METR's subsequent account of selection and concurrent-agent measurement difficulties further motivates separate labour and elapsed-time records \cite{metr2026}. The calculations here use no data from these studies.

Sections~\ref{sec:observation} and~\ref{sec:accounting} explain why commits alone need not determine person-hours and define labour, acceptance time and retained output. Section~\ref{sec:sharp-identification} derives the minimum and maximum possible labour totals, and Section~\ref{sec:estimation} uses sampled labour to correct fixed telemetry predictions. Section~\ref{sec:study} sets out how to validate these measurements before comparing AI tools and working practices. The proofs apply standard optimisation and sampling results to the stated constraints on allocating people's time. Appendix~\ref{app:scalar} gives a shortest-path solution for one person's work of one type on one task.

\section{Why event telemetry alone need not identify labour}
\label{sec:observation}

Let a minute-indexed hidden variable $Z_t\in\{0,1\}$ indicate an activity state, and let $C_t\in\{0,1\}$ record a commit event. A fitted event model specifies how activity changes from minute to minute and how activity produces commits. Interpreting $Z_t=1$ as ``coding'' requires validation against independently measured labour. Even before that validation, different probability distributions of activity can produce the same distribution of commit events.

\begin{proposition}[Failure of unrestricted identification]
\label{prop:nonidentification}
Suppose the states $Z_t$ are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter $\pi\in(0,1)$, and, independently across minutes and of the states, $B_t$ are Bernoulli variables with parameter $c\in(0,1)$. Define $C_t=Z_tB_t$. The joint distribution of all commit observations depends on $(\pi,c)$ only through $\pi c$. In particular, $(\pi,c)=(1/2,1/5)$ and $(1/4,2/5)$ give identical commit distributions but different expected active times.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each $C_t$ is Bernoulli with parameter $\theta=\pi c$, and the $C_t$ are independent. For every $n\geq1$ and every binary string $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:event-law}
 \Prb(C_1=e_1,\ldots,C_n=e_n)
 =\theta^{\sum_t e_t}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum_t e_t}.
\end{equation}
Both parameter pairs give $\theta=1/10$. In contrast,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:activity-total}
 \E\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_t\right]=n\pi,
\end{equation}
which is $n/2$ minutes in the first model and $n/4$ in the second. Thus identical commit distributions can correspond to different expected numbers of active minutes.
\end{proof}

This construction belongs to the independent-state subfamily of a two-state hidden Markov model: the next activity state has the same distribution for either current state. The example shows that different parameter values in the unrestricted model family can give the same distribution of commits. Models with persistent activity or other restrictions may still be identifiable. Restrictions on the probability model for activity, informative code-change features or independent activity observations may distinguish models that commit timing alone cannot distinguish. A claim that the parameters are identifiable must state which restrictions or observations make that possible. Under AI assistance, commits can continue while human attention is elsewhere, so the mechanism that produces commits may itself change.

Agreement between models does not establish that their predictions agree with independently measured person-hours. Measured interval totals instead constrain how much labour occurred during a specified period.

\section{Task accounting with explicit boundaries}
\label{sec:accounting}

\subsection{Tasks, AI tools and person-hours}

Begin tracking task $i$ at time $s_i$, when its requested behaviour, conditions for inclusion in the study and acceptance criteria are recorded. Identify each task by its requirement, so changing its commits or pull requests does not change which task is counted as output. Before observing outcomes, define how split tasks, merged tasks and scope changes will be handled. When a task is split, the original task can retain its identifier while separate records describe work on its components. Counting both the original task and its components as delivered output would count the same work twice. Keep a history of changes to requirements and acceptance criteria, and state how tasks with those changes will be analysed.

Before comparing AI tools and working practices, specify what counts as one task, whether accepted components count separately or only as part of a completed parent task, and when follow-up starts. Consider separately accepted components regrouped into a parent task that is accepted only after all its components have been accepted. Even when component reference scores add to the parent's score, regrouping can change how much output is counted by a deadline. The comparison therefore depends on how tasks are grouped and accepted as well as how they are scored.

For each comparison group, specify the available AI tools and model versions, the tasks on which they may be used, the actions they may perform autonomously, who reviews their work and the requirements for release. Record actual tool use separately from the tools and working practices specified for that group: difficult tasks may elicit more use, and people may decline to use a tool made available to them.

Let $a_{ijr}(t)$ be the fraction of person $j$'s time spent on task $i$ doing work of type $r$, such as implementation or review, at calendar time $t$. With finite sets of tasks, people and types of work, measurable allocation functions define cumulative labour through task age $u\geq0$, measured from the start of tracking, by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:labour}
 a_{ijr}(t)\geq0,\qquad \sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1,\qquad
 H_i(u)=\sum_{j,r}\int_{s_i}^{s_i+u}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt.
\end{equation}
With time in hours, $H_i(u)$ has units of person-hours. Include all the work on the task, from specification and implementation through review, testing and repair. Prompting and integration also consume person-hours. Pair work can contribute two person-hours in one elapsed hour. For one person, two concurrent sessions cannot each receive a full hour of attention. State whether attended waiting belongs in the labour total. Record unattended tool execution as computing-resource use.

To measure the task labour defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:labour}, specify how person-hours spent on shared review and platform work are attributed to tasks. Record separately any work that the observations cannot attribute to a particular task. Define what counts as one agent instance and the categories of computing resource used when reporting its running time. Record those running times, token use and charges separately from person-hours. Combining these quantities requires an explicit economic conversion and changes the question from labour to cost.

Let $T_i$ be elapsed time from the start of tracking to first acceptance. Starting this clock at $s_i$ includes queueing and any reasoning and specification between $s_i$ and the first commit. If the task is permanently abandoned before acceptance, put $T_i=\infty$. Abandonment is an outcome, whereas loss of observation is censoring. Record deployment separately whenever acceptance and deployment differ.

\subsection{Scoring tasks and checking retained output}

Calculate a nonnegative reference score $S_i^0$ for each task, recording which version of the scoring rule is used and which task information it takes as input. The score may count tasks or functional units, or use SCH weights. Retained output has the same unit as this score. Productivity divides that output by person-hours. The superscript $0$ denotes the common reference: retain each task's score and use the same scoring rule and weights across comparison groups. A score expressed in person-hours needs calibration against independently measured labour. Specify whose labour is used for calibration, which types of work are counted, the quality requirements and the event at which labour recording ends. A predictor trained on imputed activity states learns to predict those states; its accuracy as a measure of person-hours still requires independent validation.

For a causal comparison with fixed task requirements, requirements recorded when tracking begins are preferable to final code differences as scoring inputs: AI can change the final code. A retrospective code score can still be reported, with its dependence on the implementation made explicit. A nonlinear scoring rule need not be additive, so splitting or squashing a change can alter its score. Score the same recorded task information once, or use a requirement-scoring rule whose component scores add to its total. A failed attempt, retry or revert must not generate another unit of retained output.

Choose an acceptance deadline at task age $\tau>0$ and a follow-up duration $\Delta\geq0$. State the criteria for judging what fraction of an accepted requirement remains satisfied. For accepted tasks, let $R_i(\Delta)\in[0,1]$ be that fraction at time $s_i+T_i+\Delta$, judged using these criteria. Retained reference output is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
 Q_i(\tau,\Delta)=\begin{cases} S_i^0 R_i(\Delta),&T_i\leq\tau,\\ 0,&T_i>\tau.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Specify how the follow-up check distinguishes an intentional replacement from a defect-driven loss and how fractional scores are calculated. Surviving lines, successful tests and absence of recorded incidents each provide observations of quality, while leaving complete correctness unestablished. Measure agreement between reviewers and document which defects the tests and follow-up criteria can detect.

For a specified finite set of tasks with positive total labour, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cohort-productivity}
 P_H(\tau,\Delta)
 =\frac{\sum_i Q_i(\tau,\Delta)}{\sum_i H_i(\tau+\Delta)}.
\end{equation}
The denominator sums labour through the same task age $\tau+\Delta$ for every task in that set. It includes labour on rejected, unfinished and abandoned tasks, together with work done after acceptance that is attributable to the task. Early acceptance leaves more time for repair within this labour window than late acceptance. Stopping repair accounting exactly $\Delta$ after each acceptance would define a different denominator. State when labour recording ends and follow every task for $\tau+\Delta$ to measure both the retained output and labour defined here.

Tasks with zero human labour can contribute retained output. The productivity ratio is defined only when the tasks' total labour is positive. Report retained output and total person-hours separately.

\section{Estimating labour from sampled allocations}
\label{sec:estimation}

The preceding bounds assume exact interval labour totals or justified lower and upper bounds on them. A probability sample instead gives an estimate that varies with the selected person-intervals. Treating that estimate as an exact interval total can rule out the true work allocation because of sampling error. The difference estimator below corrects telemetry predictions using sampled labour. Under explicit sampling and measurement assumptions, its average over repeated samples equals the true labour total.

Activity sampling estimates labour from selected person-intervals. The sampling frame---the complete list of intervals from which the sample is drawn---must cover all target work, including off-keyboard reasoning, review and coordination. Short diaries and task records can show how much of each selected interval a person spent on the target work. For a subsample, state who checks the recorded allocations, what evidence they use and how they remain independent of the original recording. Report disagreement. Nonresponse, inaccurate recall and work outside the frame require separate investigation.

\section{Validating the measurements}
\label{sec:study}

An empirical study should first test whether the proposed observations measure person-hours accurately and cover all the included work. Before collection, state which tasks and types of work are included and how person-hours are attributed to tasks. For that population, specify the sampling frame, reference scoring rule, acceptance deadline and criteria for checking retained output. Measure disagreement over recorded allocations. Also record nonresponse, follow-up completeness and the time spent collecting records. Report these measurements by comparison group, type of work and task category, stating how tasks are categorised. Use these observations to choose how many person-intervals to sample and how precisely a later study should estimate differences between the groups.

For interval bounds, use independently checked totals with justified limits on measurement error. For target windows chosen before observing outcomes, compare the sums of separately calculated lower and upper labour bounds with the joint minimum and maximum. This shows whether accounting jointly for each person's available time raises the minimum or lowers the maximum. For sampled labour, compare the variance over repeated samples with and without predictions calculated before sampling and kept unchanged whichever sample is drawn. Retain the inclusion probabilities and joint sampling-design information needed to calculate uncertainty.

\subsection{Conditions for comparing AI tools and working practices}

A subsequent causal study must define whether it concerns a fixed set of tasks or an organisation's incoming work that meets stated conditions for inclusion. AI adoption can change which work is requested or undertaken. For a fixed-workload comparison, set the conditions for including tasks before allocating tasks, people or teams to comparison groups, and keep those conditions unchanged. State how work done before group allocation is counted. Record postponed, rejected and abandoned requests. An organisation-wide adoption comparison instead needs a common calendar window and records of incoming requests and work in progress. State which population each comparison concerns and how its observations are weighted.

The AI tools and working practices specified for one team can affect another team through shared reviewers, limited computing resources for agents or reusable components. Account for these effects when forming comparison groups and analysing outcomes. Analyse outcomes by the original allocation to comparison groups, recording actual tool use separately. Calculate uncertainty using the same teams, people or other units that were allocated to those groups, with additional allowance for sampling labour and checking quality in subsets. Recording more commits does not increase the number of independently allocated units.

Use the same criteria and follow-up procedure for checking retained output across comparison groups. Have two reviewers check a subset independently to measure disagreement between them. Record any loss of reviewer blinding---reviewers learning which group a task belongs to---and the timing of feedback, since the check can trigger repair. Count that repair within the stated labour window. Estimating retained output for all tasks from a checked subset requires known selection probabilities and assumptions addressing any effect of the check on subsequent work. Report severe defects separately because a retained fraction cannot describe every security or reliability consequence.

Complete follow-up through $\tau+\Delta$ as required by Section~\ref{sec:accounting}. Distinguish permanent abandonment from loss of observation. If follow-up is incomplete, state the assumptions or bounds used to handle missing outcomes. Report labour, acceptance and retained output together, since reduced labour may accompany more abandonment. Report retained output and positive total labour alongside their productivity ratio, and account for uncertainty in both quantities and their dependence. Prespecify practically useful improvement thresholds and any maximum acceptable reduction in quality (the noninferiority margin). Failure to detect a quality reduction does not establish that the loss is within that limit.

\section{Scope and next observations}
\label{sec:limits}

Different distributions of human activity can produce identical commit distributions. Interval totals or justified bounds, together with each person's available time, restrict how much work could have occurred during a specified period. Splitting time at every observation endpoint and target-window boundary gives a finite optimisation problem with exactly the same possible labour totals. Adding separately calculated interval bounds can include totals that would require someone to work beyond their available time. The summed lower and upper bounds are exact only if work allocations achieving the respective component bounds can occur together.

Probability sampling permits design-unbiased estimation when the frame covers the target work, inclusion probabilities are known and positive, and sampled allocations are measured accurately. A fixed telemetry predictor improves precision only when estimating its total residual has lower sampling variance than estimating labour without predictions. Diaries and task records can supply the observations. Whether they capture reasoning and coordination and accurately record how people divide their time requires empirical evidence. The unbiasedness and precision results depend on the stated measurement and sampling assumptions. Establishing a causal benefit from AI adoption requires a further comparison of AI tools and working practices.

The next empirical test is whether task records can account for person-hours spent on all included types of work and independently checked retained output through the required follow-up. Disagreement over recorded allocations, missing records and the time needed to collect them will help determine how much measurement is practicable before a causal study is designed. That study must also choose whether to compare a fixed workload or organisation-wide adoption that can change incoming requirements. The results specify which labour totals are consistent with the observations, when sampling gives an unbiased estimate and when fixed predictions reduce its variance.
