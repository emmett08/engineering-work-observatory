\begin{abstract}
Software-work telemetry records events whose relationship to human labour can change under AI assistance. We determine which labour totals are compatible with specified observations, keeping elapsed time to acceptance and retained output as separate endpoints. Exact interval totals and a per-person capacity constraint yield attainable labour bounds at a requested cutoff. For overlapping observations and shared capacity, partitioning time at observation endpoints and target cutoffs reduces the continuous allocation problem exactly to a finite linear programme. Its extrema delimit all attainable totals under the declared observations. Adding separate interval bounds can give a wider range. When labour is probability-sampled, fixed telemetry predictions can assist estimation through a design-unbiased residual correction. Its variance depends on prediction residuals, so inaccurate predictions can reduce precision. The results require no probabilistic execution model. We supply analytical proofs and exact finite computational checks. No organisational data are analysed. The proposed measurements still require empirical validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the observations determine}
\label{sec:introduction}

Suppose a team records three person-hours of work during a four-hour interval. How much occurred during its first two hours? If one person supplied the work, the answer lies between one and two person-hours: at most two fit after the cutoff, and at most two fit before it. Both limits are attainable. The interval total therefore identifies a range without identifying the allocation within it. Commit times alone may supply even less information, because the same event sequence can arise from different amounts of human activity.

The engineering question is how much labour the observations determine, and which additional measurements would narrow the answer. We address it for AI-assisted software work using all-role person-hours, elapsed time to first acceptance and reference output retained at a stated follow-up. These definitions account for unsuccessful work, concurrent activity and post-acceptance repair. The principal result represents all feasible labour totals by a finite linear programme whose constraints encode interval totals or bounds and shared per-person capacity. Minimising and maximising the desired total gives its identified range: every value between the extrema is attainable by an admissible allocation. Narrower bounds therefore require additional observations or justified restrictions on those allocations.

When exact allocation totals are unavailable, probability sampling supports design-unbiased estimates through known inclusion probabilities. Fixed telemetry predictions can improve precision if their residuals reduce variance under the sampling design. This creates a useful role for telemetry even when its events leave labour unidentified: allocation measurements collected under a probability design can correct prediction error. Deterministic identification intervals, sampling uncertainty and uncertainty in a causal intervention comparison consequently answer different questions.

Wright and Ziegler's Standard Coder approach \cite{wright2019} motivates the observation problem. It infers coding activity from commit timing and predicts the resulting labels from code-change features. Standard Coding Hours (SCH) can serve as a common reference index, but calibration to independently observed labour requires a separate argument. AI may alter both the event-to-activity relationship and the work represented by a code change. The SPACE framework places activity and efficiency within a broader account of developer productivity \cite{forsgren2021}. The CUPS taxonomy examines interaction time costs in AI-assisted programming \cite{mozannar2024}. Our narrower contribution is the labour identified or estimable under an explicit observation scheme, with separate definitions for elapsed time and retained output.

Existing AI studies address different parts of this measurement problem. Peng et al.\ examine completion time among completers of a programming exercise \cite{peng2023}. Becker et al.\ study experienced maintainers in familiar repositories with a forecast-adjusted log-time contrast \cite{becker2025}. Cui et al.\ study weekly completed pull requests in organisational experiments \cite{cui2026}, with assignment used as an instrument for adoption in their June 2025 manuscript \cite[Section 4.1 and Table 3]{cui2025manuscript}. These differences in populations, interventions and outcomes prevent treating their estimates as interchangeable parameters. METR's subsequent account of selection and concurrent-agent measurement difficulties further motivates separate labour and elapsed-time records \cite{metr2026}. We use these studies to locate the measurement questions. They supply no data for the calculations here.

Section~\ref{sec:sharp-identification} derives the labour bounds, and Section~\ref{sec:estimation} connects sampled labour to telemetry-assisted estimation. Section~\ref{sec:study} sets out the measurement validation needed before an intervention comparison. The finite reduction and attainability arguments specialise standard optimisation and sampling results to the stated allocation constraints. \section{Why event telemetry alone need not identify labour}
\label{sec:observation}

Let a minute-indexed hidden variable $Z_t\in\{0,1\}$ indicate an activity state, and let $C_t\in\{0,1\}$ record a commit event. A fitted event model specifies both activity dynamics and the mechanism that produces commits from activity. Interpreting $Z_t=1$ as ``coding'' requires validation against independently measured labour. Even before that validation, distinct activity laws can produce the same event law.

\begin{proposition}[Failure of unrestricted identification]
\label{prop:nonidentification}
Suppose the states $Z_t$ are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter $\pi\in(0,1)$, and, independently across minutes and of the states, $B_t$ are Bernoulli variables with parameter $c\in(0,1)$. Define $C_t=Z_tB_t$. The complete law of the event process depends on $(\pi,c)$ only through $\pi c$. In particular, $(\pi,c)=(1/2,1/5)$ and $(1/4,2/5)$ give identical observed laws but different expected active times.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each $C_t$ is Bernoulli with parameter $\theta=\pi c$, and the $C_t$ are independent. For every $n\geq1$ and every binary string $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:event-law}
 \Prb(C_1=e_1,\ldots,C_n=e_n)
 =\theta^{\sum_t e_t}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum_t e_t}.
\end{equation}
Both parameter pairs give $\theta=1/10$. In contrast,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:activity-total}
 \E\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_t\right]=n\pi,
\end{equation}
which is $n/2$ minutes in the first model and $n/4$ in the second. Thus the same observable distribution corresponds to two different target values.
\end{proof}

This construction belongs to the independent-state subfamily of a two-state hidden Markov model: both transition rows give the same next-state probability. It shows that the unrestricted family is not globally identifiable. Persistent or otherwise restricted models may still be identifiable. Additional restrictions, informative code marks or independent activity observations may distinguish the relevant cases. An identification claim must state the restrictions or observations on which it depends. AI makes the question more immediate because commit production can continue while human attention is elsewhere.

Agreement between model predictions leaves their calibration to independently observed labour untested. We next ask how interval observations constrain labour.

\section{Task accounting with explicit boundaries}
\label{sec:accounting}

\subsection{The task, the assigned intervention and the person-hour}

Enrol task $i$ at time $s_i$, when its requested behaviour, eligibility and acceptance criteria are recorded. Give the task an identity tied to its requirement, so commits and pull requests can change without changing the unit counted. Before observing outcomes, define how split tasks, merged tasks and scope changes will be handled. A parent requirement can retain its original identity while children record implementation activity. Counting both the parent and its children as delivered output would count the same work twice. Changes affecting the stated requirement or acceptance criteria require a versioned record and a stated analysis rule.

That rule must also fix task granularity, acceptance credit and the follow-up clock before interventions are compared. Even additive reference scores can yield different deadline output when separately accepted components are regrouped into a parent accepted only after its last component. Freezing the score alone therefore leaves part of the comparison unspecified.

An \emph{AI intervention} specifies available tools and model versions, eligible tasks, permitted autonomous actions, review responsibilities and release requirements. A comparison of interventions must specify what is assigned and record actual use separately, since difficult tasks may elicit more use and people may decline an assigned tool.

Let $a_{ijr}(t)$ be the fraction of person $j$'s time allocated to task $i$ in role $r$ at time $t$. For a finite set of observed people, measurable allocation functions satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:labour}
 a_{ijr}(t)\geq0,\qquad \sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1,\qquad
 H_i(u)=\sum_{j,r}\int_{s_i}^{s_i+u}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt.
\end{equation}
With time in hours, $H_i(u)$ has units of person-hours. Roles include specification, implementation, prompting, review, testing, integration and repair. Pair work can contribute two person-hours in one elapsed hour. For one person, two concurrent sessions cannot each receive a full hour of attention. State whether attended waiting belongs in the labour total. Unattended execution is recorded as agent resource use.

Equation~\eqref{eq:labour} defines the target, while the observation procedure determines how precisely its allocations are known. Shared review and platform work therefore need an attribution rule, with an overhead category where the observations cannot justify allocation to particular tasks. Record agent instance-hours by resource class, tokens and charges separately from person-hours. Combining these resources requires an explicit economic conversion and changes the question from labour to cost.

Let $T_i$ be elapsed time from enrolment to first acceptance. Starting this clock at enrolment includes queueing and any reasoning and specification between enrolment and the first commit. If the task is permanently abandoned before acceptance, put $T_i=\infty$. Abandonment is an outcome, whereas loss of observation is censoring. Record deployment separately whenever acceptance and deployment differ.

\subsection{A frozen reference and a follow-up assessment}

Assign a nonnegative reference score $S_i^0$ using a declared scoring-rule version and task representation. It may count tasks or functional units, or use frozen SCH weights. The superscript $0$ denotes the reference held fixed across interventions. A score expressed in person-hours needs calibration to independently measured labour for a specified reference population, included roles, quality criterion and stopping rule. A predictor trained on imputed activity states targets those states, leaving its calibration to labour untested.

For a fixed-scope causal comparison, requirements recorded at intake are preferable to final code differences: AI can change the latter. A retrospective code score can still be reported, with its dependence on the implementation made explicit. A nonlinear scoring rule need not be additive, so splitting or squashing a change can alter its score. Score a canonical task representation once, or use an additive requirement rubric. A failed attempt, retry or revert must not generate another unit of retained output.

Choose an acceptance horizon $\tau>0$ and a follow-up duration $\Delta\geq0$. For accepted tasks, let $R_i(\Delta)\in[0,1]$ be the assessed fraction of the accepted requirement still meeting the rubric at time $s_i+T_i+\Delta$. Retained reference output is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
 Q_i(\tau,\Delta)=\begin{cases} S_i^0 R_i(\Delta),&T_i\leq\tau,\\ 0,&T_i>\tau.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The rubric must distinguish an intentional replacement from a defect-driven loss and explain fractional scores. Surviving lines, successful tests and absence of recorded incidents are observations of quality. None establishes complete correctness. The study must assess agreement between reviewers and document the limitations of its tests and rubric.

For a finite intake cohort with positive total labour, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cohort-productivity}
 P_H(\tau,\Delta)
 =\frac{\sum_i Q_i(\tau,\Delta)}{\sum_i H_i(\tau+\Delta)}.
\end{equation}
The denominator is total cohort labour through the same task age $\tau+\Delta$ for every enrolment. It includes labour on rejected, unfinished and abandoned tasks, together with task-attributable work after acceptance. An early acceptance leaves more of this horizon remaining than a late one. Stopping each task's repair accounting exactly $\Delta$ after acceptance therefore defines a different labour boundary. Either convention can be useful, but the choice must be explicit. Follow every enrolment for $\tau+\Delta$ to ascertain the numerator and this denominator.

Tasks with zero human labour can contribute output to the cohort ratio. The ratio is defined when aggregate labour is positive. Report its numerator and denominator separately.

\section{Validating the measurements}
\label{sec:study}

An empirical study should first test whether the proposed observations can be collected accurately and with the required coverage. Register the task scope, attribution rules, sampling frame, reference score, acceptance horizon and retention rubric before collection. Measure allocation disagreement, nonresponse, follow-up completeness and collection burden by intervention, role and task class. Use these observations to choose sampling intensity and a precision target for a later intervention comparison.

For interval bounds, use independently assessed totals with justified ascertainment-error limits. Compare the width of marginal bounds with the joint range under shared capacity at prespecified cutoffs. Treating sampled estimates as exact totals changes the guarantee and may exclude the true allocation. For sampled labour, compare the design variance of estimates with and without predictions frozen before sampling. Preserve the inclusion probabilities and joint-design information needed for uncertainty calculations.

\subsection{Conditions for an intervention comparison}

A subsequent causal study must define whether it concerns a fixed workload or an eligible portfolio whose intake can change under the intervention. Freeze task eligibility and the treatment of pre-assignment work for a fixed-workload comparison. Record postponed, rejected and abandoned requests. An organisation-wide adoption comparison instead needs a common calendar window and records of intake and work in progress. State the population and weighting for each contrast.

Assignment must account for shared reviewers, agent capacity and reusable components. If those resources connect teams, randomising teams separately leaves cross-team effects that the analysis must address. Analyse tasks by assigned intervention, retain the assignment units in uncertainty calculations, and account for labour and quality subsampling. The number of commits supplies no additional independent assignments.

Use the same retention rubric and follow-up procedure across intervention groups. Duplicate assessment of a subset measures disagreement. Record failures of reviewer blinding and the timing of feedback, since assessment can trigger repair. Count that repair within the declared labour window. Extrapolating from an assessed subset requires known selection probabilities and assumptions addressing any effect of assessment on subsequent work. Report severe defects separately because a retained fraction cannot represent every security or reliability consequence.

Complete follow-up through $\tau+\Delta$ as required by Section~\ref{sec:accounting}. Distinguish permanent abandonment from loss of observation. If follow-up is incomplete, state the assumptions or bounds used to handle missing outcomes. Report labour, acceptance and retained output together, since reduced labour may accompany more abandonment. A productivity ratio needs its numerator, positive denominator and their joint uncertainty. Prespecify practical improvement thresholds and any quality noninferiority margin. Failure to detect a quality reduction does not establish that the margin is met.

\section{Scope and next observations}
\label{sec:limits}

The observation scheme determines which labour totals telemetry can identify. Commit events admit observationally equivalent activity laws. Exact interval masses and per-person capacity yield attainable cutoff bounds. Partitioning at every observation endpoint and target cutoff preserves all constraints needed to compute the complete identified range by finite optimisation. Separate interval bounds remain valid under their observation premises, but can omit restrictions imposed by shared capacity. An implementation that adds those bounds therefore reports a conservative range unless joint attainability is established.

Sampling adds uncertainty about the labour represented by unobserved intervals. Known inclusion probabilities give unbiased totals under accurate allocation ascertainment. A fixed telemetry predictor improves precision only when its residuals reduce design variance. When diaries supply allocation observations, their accuracy and coverage of reasoning and coordination require separate evidence. The sampling frame must also cover the target work. Identification ranges remain conditional on the measurements and allocation constraints. Design unbiasedness remains conditional on the sampling and ascertainment premises. A causal benefit from AI adoption requires a further comparison of interventions.

The measurement pilot should first establish whether task identities can connect all-role allocations to independently assessed retained output through the required follow-up. It should report ascertainment disagreement, missingness and collection burden so that a later intervention trial can choose its sampling intensity and precision target. A fixed-task trial and an organisation-wide adoption study address different quantities when the intervention changes incoming requirements. The present results specify which quantities the available observations determine and which remain bounded or unidentified.

\section*{Reproducibility}

The repository at \url{https://github.com/emmett08/engineering-work-observatory/tree/main/paper} contains the LaTeX source, exact rational interval checker and finite sampling checks. The analytical proofs establish the general results. The executable checks compare finite constructed cases with those results and do not validate workplace measurements. The application implements marginal labour bounds and the fixed-prediction estimator. It does not solve the joint shared-capacity programme. Its floating-point bounds have no outward-rounding guarantee, and its variance estimate is limited to the declared simple-random-sampling case. Input checks cannot establish frame completeness, allocation accuracy or compliance with a sampling design.

The unchanged historical package retains Lean proofs of the finite observation-law counterexample and inverse-probability-weighting algebra. Its proof receipt covers neither the attainable interval bounds nor the joint cell reduction, difference-estimator variance or sampling-validation arguments developed here. Those results have the analytical proofs and computational checks described above.
