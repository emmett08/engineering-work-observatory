\title[Human labour in AI-assisted software engineering]{Bounding and estimating human labour in AI-assisted software engineering}
\date{19 November 2026}
\keywords{software engineering, partial identification, labour bounds, activity sampling, model-assisted estimation, AI assistance}
\hypersetup{pdftitle={Bounding and estimating human labour in AI-assisted software engineering},pdfsubject={Labour bounds from interval observations and estimation from probability samples},pdfkeywords={software engineering, labour bounds, activity sampling, model-assisted estimation}}
\begin{document}
\begin{abstract}
Engineers can spend different amounts of time producing the same sequence of commits. Given observations of labour over overlapping periods, a finite linear programme finds the least and greatest person-hours compatible with those totals and each contributor's available time. If the constraints admit a schedule and work can stop and resume at any time, every total between the limits is attainable.

In 96 constructed two-person schedules, enforcing the engineer's shared time limit reduced the mean width of the first-hour labour range from 1.5 to 0.5 person-hours. Omitting the reviewer excluded the true total in 26 schedules. For accurately measured intervals drawn with known, positive selection probabilities from a frame covering all eligible hours, inverse-probability weighting estimates total labour without bias. In a separate comparison of six constructed team pairs, omitted review time and inflated requirement scores together produced a false declaration of benefit from AI access. A workplace trial needs independent evidence about both errors in each group.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the observations determine}
\label{sec:introduction}

An engineer spends three hours on a requirement during a four-hour period. At most two hours fit in the second half, so at least one hour of work must fall in the first. The engineer could also have worked throughout the first half and for one hour in the second. The same three-hour total permits either schedule. A narrower range requires evidence about when the work took place.

Even the total is uncertain when only commit times are available. An engineer can discuss a release without making a commit, and an artificial intelligence (AI) workflow can commit a change while the engineer reviews another task. We define \emph{labour} as the person-hours devoted to the requirements under study. Abandoning more requirements could reduce that total, so interpreting a labour saving also requires the time to first acceptance and, for timely acceptances, the fraction of the requested behaviour the software still provides at follow-up.

Perry et al.\ compared software developers' time diaries with direct observation \cite{perry1994}. A diary may establish how long an activity took within a shift while leaving its start and finish uncertain. Preemptive scheduling divides time at job release dates and deadlines to determine how much processing fits between them \cite{bertsimas2009flows}. We use the boundaries of observed periods to calculate how much completed work could fall within a chosen window. This accommodates overlapping observations, including totals across several tasks or people. When a team measures labour in a probability sample of intervals, the generalised difference estimator \cite{cassel1976} uses those measurements to correct predictions for the full sampling frame. The correction improves precision if estimating the total prediction error has lower sampling variance than estimating total labour directly.

Wright and Ziegler's Standard Coder method infers coding activity from commit times, then uses code changes to predict the resulting labels \cite{wright2019}. Agreement with those labels measures how closely the predictor reproduces the activity model. To interpret its predictions as person-hours, we need independent measurements of the labour that went into the changes. Comparing those measurements across toolsets matters if AI assistance alters how much work a commit represents. Forsgren et al.\ distinguish developer activity from efficiency \cite{forsgren2021}, and Mozannar et al.\ examine the time programmers spend interacting with AI tools \cite{mozannar2024}.

The choice of outcome matters in studies of AI assistance. Peng et al.\ measured completion time among participants who finished a programming exercise \cite{peng2023}. If more participants abandon the exercise, a shorter time among finishers can accompany fewer completions. Becker et al.\ compared log completion times for experienced maintainers in familiar repositories, adjusting for their forecasts of task duration \cite{becker2025}. Cui et al.\ counted completed pull requests per week in organisational experiments \cite{cui2026}. In their June 2025 manuscript they use random allocation to study groups as an instrument for adoption \cite[Section 4.1 and Table 3]{cui2025manuscript}. The number of completed pull requests also depends on how a team divides a requirement into changes. Model Evaluation and Threat Research describe difficulties selecting participants and measuring time during concurrent agent sessions \cite{metr2026}.

We first establish what commit times identify (Section~\ref{sec:observation}), then define the contributors, tasks and observation windows for a study of labour (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}). Interval totals yield bounds on person-hours (Section~\ref{sec:sharp-identification}). A probability sample yields an estimate with sampling uncertainty (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}). We test both calculations on constructed schedules with known labour in Section~\ref{sec:known-labour}. Section~\ref{sec:study} examines how measurement error affects the evaluation of forecasts and a proposed trial of AI access. Appendix~\ref{app:scalar} gives a shortest-path calculation for one person doing one type of work on one task.

\section{What commit times identify about activity}
\label{sec:observation}

Consider a model in which each active minute has a chance of producing a commit. Let $Z_t=1$ if the engineer is active in minute $t$, with $Z_t=0$ otherwise. The observer sees only $C_t$, an indicator of whether a commit occurred during that minute.\footnote{For an event $A$, $\Prb(A)$ denotes its probability. For a random variable $X$, $\E[X]$ denotes its expectation. A Bernoulli variable takes the value one with the specified probability and zero otherwise.} The same commit frequency is compatible with a lower chance of a commit in each active minute and more active minutes.

\begin{proposition}[Failure of unrestricted identification]
\label{prop:nonidentification}
Suppose the activity indicators $Z_t$ are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter $\pi\in(0,1)$. Let $B_t$ be Bernoulli variables with parameter $c\in(0,1)$, independent across minutes and independent of the entire activity sequence $(Z_t)$. Define $C_t=Z_tB_t$. The probability of any finite sequence of commit observations depends on $(\pi,c)$ only through $\pi c$. The choices $(\pi,c)=(1/2,1/5)$ and $(1/4,2/5)$ give the same distribution of commits and different expected amounts of active time.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The $C_t$ are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter $\theta=\pi c$. For every $n\geq1$ and every binary string $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:event-law}
 \Prb(C_1=e_1,\ldots,C_n=e_n)
 =\theta^{\sum_t e_t}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum_t e_t}.
\end{equation}
Both parameter pairs give $\theta=1/10$. The expected number of active minutes is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:activity-total}
 \E\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_t\right]=n\pi,
\end{equation}
which gives $n/2$ for the first pair and $n/4$ for the second.
\end{proof}

Over an hour, each model predicts six commits on average. They imply 30 and 15 minutes of expected activity, respectively. Equation~\eqref{eq:event-law} applies to sequences of any finite length, so collecting more commits cannot distinguish these two explanations.

This example belongs to the class of hidden Markov models. Its probability of activity in the next minute is $\pi$ regardless of current activity. A model in which activity persists across adjacent minutes may exclude one of the two parameter pairs. For finite hidden Markov models, generic identification permits exceptional parameter choices and permutations of the hidden labels \cite[Section~6.1]{allman2009}.\footnote{In a hidden Markov model, the current hidden value determines the distribution of the next one. Given the hidden sequence, the observations are independent and each depends only on its corresponding hidden value. Generic identification means that, outside an exceptional set, the distribution of observations determines the parameters up to relabelling.} Additional observations, such as features of code changes, could distinguish the two models if their distributions differ between the parameter pairs.

Interpreting $Z_t=1$ as a minute of human work requires evidence about the engineer even when commit times identify the model parameters. An unattended agent, for example, can create a commit. Observations of the contributor's time can bound labour once the study specifies which task and period each observation covers.

\section{Defining task labour and retained output}
\label{sec:accounting}

\subsection{Whose time counts towards a task}

Task $i$ enters the study at time $s_i$, when it meets the inclusion rules and its description specifies the requested behaviour and acceptance criteria. Intake starts the clocks for labour and time to acceptance. Engineers may already have elicited the request and drafted its criteria. A study of that earlier work would follow requests from arrival, including those that never reach intake. Here the workload comprises the requirements selected at intake. Each keeps its identity as commits and pull requests change. Keep every version of the requirement so reviewers can apply a scoring rule chosen in advance for scope changes, splits and mergers.

When a requirement splits, link its components to the original identifier. A team can credit each completed component or award the parent score only when the whole requirement is complete. Consider two components, one accepted before the deadline and one afterwards. The first earns credit under component scoring. The parent earns zero under the whole-requirement rule, even if its initial score equals the sum of the two component scores. Specify the unit of credit before observing the outcomes and count each contribution once.

For each task class and comparison group, document which AI tools and model versions people could use. Identify the actions a tool can take on its own and those that require review or a release check. Track actual use separately from access. Engineers may seek help on hard tasks. Comparing tasks on which they used the tool with those on which they worked unaided can therefore confound the effect of access with task difficulty.

Concurrent AI workflows may draw on the same engineer. At calendar time $t$, let $a_{ijr}(t)$ be the fraction of person $j$'s time devoted to activity $r$ on task $i$, where $r$ might denote implementation or review. At most one hour of a person's time is available across all activities in a clock hour. For finite sets of tasks, people and activities, and measurable allocation functions,\footnote{Integration accumulates the allocation over elapsed time. A constant fraction $a$ over $e-s$ hours contributes $a(e-s)$ person-hours. We use Lebesgue integration with respect to time. Measurability ensures that a set of times defined by an allocation threshold has a duration. A condition that holds almost everywhere can fail on a set of duration zero, such as individual switching instants, without affecting these integrals.} the labour through task age $u\geq0$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:labour}
 a_{ijr}(t)\geq0,\qquad \sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1,\qquad
 H_i(u)=\sum_{j,r}\int_{s_i}^{s_i+u}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt.
\end{equation}
With time in hours, $H_i(u)$ counts person-hours. Two engineers reviewing the same change for one hour contribute two person-hours. One engineer supervising two workflows divides their attention between them.

In Figure~\ref{fig:human-and-workflow-time}, one engineer spends minutes 0--10 preparing prompt A and minutes 10--20 preparing prompt B. Each workflow starts when the engineer submits its prompt. Workflow A runs until minute 60. Workflow B finishes at minute 40, and the engineer spends the remaining twenty minutes reviewing its result. Assume the two tasks require no other human attention during the hour. Human labour totals forty minutes, or $2/3$ of a person-hour. The workflows run for fifty and twenty minutes respectively, giving seventy minutes of execution, or $7/6$ workflow-hours.

Post-intake labour covers clarification of the requirement through implementation, testing and repair. Specify when waiting for a workflow demands attention and how contributors divide shared review or platform work among the tasks it supports. If an engineer helped with an unknown task, keep the hours with their attribution unresolved. Measure workflow-hours by defining the start and finish of each run and summing elapsed execution inside the observation window. Concurrent runs each contribute their full duration within the window. To compare costs, price the person-hours and the computing resources consumed, using the applicable unit for each charge, such as CPU-hours or tokens.

Let $T_i$ be the elapsed time from $s_i$ to first acceptance. It includes queueing and unattended execution as well as time spent on the task. For permanent abandonment before acceptance, set $T_i=\infty$. If observation ends while acceptance remains possible, only the time to the last observation is known. Use the time of first acceptance even when deployment takes place later.

\subsection{Scoring requirements at intake and follow-up}

Let $S_i^0\geq0$ be the reference score of requirement $i$ at intake. Giving every task one unit weights tasks equally. Scoring functional units gives a requirement with more units a larger share of the total. A team using Standard Coding Hours as weights must specify their units and the inputs to the calculation. Apply the same scoring rule to both groups and retain the inputs for each requirement. Its initial weight then stays fixed however many person-hours delivery takes.

A score derived from final code can depend on the implementation chosen under AI access. Disclose whether AI access can thereby change both labour and the task's weight in the output measure. Splitting or squashing a change can alter a nonlinear score even when behaviour is identical. Score the original description once, or use additive component weights. Credit each delivered requirement once across retries and reverts.

A requirement's weight sets its contribution to the retained score. A labour forecast expresses the hours the task will take. To use Standard Coding Hours as a prediction of person-hours through a given task age, compare the prediction with independently measured hours for the same work and period. Section~\ref{sec:labour-prediction-validation} gives a benchmark using labour on earlier tasks.

Set a deadline at task age $\tau>0$. A task accepted by then is due for a follow-up assessment $\Delta\geq0$ time units after first acceptance. Let $R_i(\Delta)\in[0,1]$ be the fraction of its original requirement still satisfied at calendar time $s_i+T_i+\Delta$, judged using criteria specified before follow-up. Its retained reference score is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
 Q_i(\tau,\Delta)=\begin{cases} S_i^0 R_i(\Delta),&T_i\leq\tau,\\ 0,&T_i>\tau.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
If a team delivers a ten-unit requirement on time and fulfils 80 per cent of it at follow-up, it earns eight units. Reviewers need criteria for partial fulfilment and a way to distinguish an intentional change of requirement from a defect. Line survival alone cannot establish which requested behaviour the software still provides. Tests cover the cases they exercise. Incident reports cover failures that someone detected and reported. Document the defects each check can reveal and measure agreement between reviewers.

For a finite cohort with positive total labour, divide total retained score by the person-hours all contributors spent on its tasks through the common task age $\tau+\Delta$. This defines productivity as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cohort-productivity}
 P_H(\tau,\Delta)
 =\frac{\sum_i Q_i(\tau,\Delta)}{\sum_i H_i(\tau+\Delta)}.
\end{equation}
A missed deadline gives zero output credit while leaving all attributable labour in the denominator. A fully automated task can contribute output with zero person-hours. If total cohort labour is zero, report each total separately since the ratio is undefined. When either total is estimated, quantify uncertainty in the ratio as described in Section~\ref{sec:difference-contract}.

The denominator specifies a period for each task, from intake through age $\tau+\Delta$. The evidence may place only limits on labour within it. A realised total is \emph{point-identified} if at least one allocation fits the evidence and every such allocation gives the same total. Proposition~\ref{prop:nonidentification} considered what a distribution of commits reveals about expected activity. For a particular history, the calculation below finds the least and greatest labour consistent with its interval observations.

\section{Estimating labour from sampled allocations}
\label{sec:estimation}

A team may measure hours for only a sample of the eligible intervals. Different draws can give different estimates of total labour. Using a sampled estimate as an exact constraint in the linear programme could exclude the hours the team actually worked. Weighting each measured contribution by the inverse of its selection probability estimates the total over the eligible intervals.

The sampling frame lists the periods in which each contributor could have worked on the requirements. It includes time away from the keyboard, whether an engineer is discussing a design or helping a colleague. A dated task note or diary entry can supply hours for a selected interval. A reviewer can check a subsample against evidence from the shift and explain any corrections. An unanswered prompt leaves hours unknown within the frame. A shift omitted from the frame has no chance of selection, so checking the list of shifts requires a separate source such as the on-call schedule.

\section{Workplace validation}
\label{sec:study}

The pilot begins by identifying everyone who contributes to an enrolled requirement. A reviewer may take part in a release discussion without committing code. An on-call engineer may spend an entire shift on an incident with no repository activity. The sampling frame must include the eligible hours of both contributors. The study also needs a rule for work shared across requirements. An engineer who remembers a review but cannot identify the ticket can still contribute a known number of hours to the cohort if every plausible task belongs to it and the review falls within each task's labour window. If only some of those tasks qualify, the labour bounds must allow for either attribution. Before collection, the team fixes each requirement's acceptance deadline and the criteria for assessing it at follow-up.

A prompt about a randomly selected, completed interval can elicit zero minutes, time divided between tasks or a description of work the contributor cannot attribute. Keeping unanswered prompts in the sample allows the team to measure response rates by role and study group. Minutes spent answering quantify the burden on each group. Comparisons by activity also require independent evidence of what happened during missed prompts. For each unresolved interval, its duration limits the hours the team could have missed. Further observations can narrow that range. Carrying the ranges through the estimate and its uncertainty calculation shows whether any admissible hours would change the conclusion.

A shift audit provides another check on the measurements. Select the shifts independently of the replies. Two accounts may agree because both omit the same design conversation. A separately timed review with confirmed attendance supplies a reference for those specific hours. For the rest of the shift, the eligible time remaining limits how much both accounts could have missed. Reporting the reference's error limits alongside the discrepancies and the cost of collection lets a team judge the precision it can afford.

To isolate the contribution of shared capacity on workplace data, use the same target window, contributors and observation limits in two calculations. First give each activity the full cell duration as its upper limit. Then restrict the sum of one person's activities to the same duration. The reduction in width measures the contribution of that restriction in person-hours. Summing separately calculated task ranges can discard information from other sources. Suppose two people on different tasks report a combined total of one hour. Either task could account for the whole hour. The observed sum fixes their combined labour.

Predictions of hours already worked can improve the sampling estimate. Their contribution is the change in sampling variance under the same design, including the probabilities of selecting intervals together where the calculation requires them. A forecast for planning must reach the team at intake. Judging its accuracy later requires an estimate of labour over that same window and an allowance for error in that estimate.

\subsection{Comparing teams with different access to AI}

Access to AI can influence which requests reach a team and which it accepts. To compare labour on a fixed workload, specify eligibility before allocating teams to groups. Include a rule for work already in progress and retain the disposition of every request, including those deferred, declined or abandoned. To estimate the effect on incoming demand, follow requests and work in progress over the same calendar period in both groups. In either design, the analysis must specify which teams or requests its estimates represent and how much each contributes.

A reviewer working with two teams can carry a technique learned with AI into the comparison group. Code reuse can spread the benefit further. Competition for computing time can instead delay other teams. These links matter when choosing which groups can be randomised independently. Analyse teams according to their original allocation and measure uptake separately. With randomisation within matched pairs, the pairs provide the independent comparisons. A thousand commits from a single team still contribute just one team to the comparison. Uncertainty in the comparison also includes any sampling of contributors' hours or of tasks for quality assessment.

Both groups need the same criteria and follow-up procedure. Independent assessments of a subsample reveal how often reviewers disagree. Disclose when a reviewer learns which group produced a change and when feedback reaches the team, since feedback can prompt repair. Repair within the labour window counts towards the cohort's hours. Estimating a cohort score from audited tasks requires their probabilities of selection and an account of any subsequent changes prompted by the audit. Report severe reliability or security failures separately. A high score on the other requirements can conceal the importance of a single failure.

Follow each task through age $\tau+\Delta$ (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}). Permanent abandonment can save hours at the cost of a failed requirement. Loss to follow-up leaves the outcome unknown and calls for a defensible range or an explicit assumption about it. Report hours alongside timely acceptance and the score at follow-up. A rate per person-hour requires positive total labour. Include both totals and, when estimated, their uncertainty and any covariance relevant to the ratio.

Before observing outcomes, the team specifies the smallest labour saving that would justify adoption and the largest tolerable loss of retained functionality. The latter sets the noninferiority margin. A claim of acceptable retention requires a lower confidence bound on the difference between the groups that exceeds the negative of this margin.

\section{What a workplace study can establish}
\label{sec:limits}

In the opening example, three hours of labour fit into a four-hour period in several ways. If a two-hour review occupied the entire second half, exactly one hour of labour fell in the first. In the 96 constructed histories, an observation across tasks likewise settled the first-hour total when combined with the reviewer's hours. Enforcing shared capacity helped when the engineer's timings were unknown. Omitting the reviewer could produce a narrow interval that excluded the hours actually worked.

The first empirical question is how much labour the proposed measurements miss. A pilot can compare contributors' accounts with independently timed activities on selected shifts, including incident response and review away from the repository. Together with the time contributors spend reporting, the reference measurements show what precision a team can obtain at that cost. Uncertainty about actual hours also limits how accurately the team can judge a forecast. In a trial of AI access, the relevant error is the difference in labour undercounted per initial reference unit between the two groups. If that difference could explain the apparent saving, recruiting more teams would leave its cause unresolved.
