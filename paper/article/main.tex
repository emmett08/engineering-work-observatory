\begin{abstract}
Artificial intelligence (AI) can change the relationship between development events, such as Git commits, and the human work behind them. We measure that work in person-hours. To relate labour to delivery, we also measure elapsed time to first acceptance and assess how much of the accepted requirement is still satisfied a specified time after first acceptance. For a task accepted by the deadline, multiply its reference score by this fulfilment fraction to obtain a weighted score. Tasks that miss the deadline receive a weighted score of zero. We examine how measured labour totals for time intervals constrain the labour possible within other periods, and how sampled labour measurements can correct predictions based on development data, such as commit histories.

Given observed labour totals or justified bounds on those totals, each person's available time limits how much labour can fall within a specified period. Dividing time at the start and end of every observed interval and every period whose labour total is sought gives a finite linear programme with exactly the same possible labour totals. This remains true when observations overlap or several tasks share a person's time. Enforcing each person's capacity across tasks can rule out totals included when separately calculated bounds are added. Whenever at least one allocation satisfies the observations and capacity limits, every total from the minimum to the maximum is possible. Each total can also be achieved by a schedule in which each person does at most one activity at a time, provided work may start and stop at arbitrary times.

For estimation, we list the time intervals for each person whose labour is included and select intervals at random, giving each a known positive probability of selection. In each selected interval, we measure labour and subtract its prediction. Adding each difference divided by its selection probability to the total predicted for all listed intervals corrects that total. Predictions are calculated before selection and remain unchanged across the possible samples. With accurate labour measurements, the corrected estimate averages to true total labour over repeated samples. Predictions can increase or decrease its variance. The results follow from proofs within the stated allocation and sampling models. Lean checks supporting results about interval integrals and the schedules used in the proofs, while exact finite calculations check numerical examples. Empirical validation of the measurements is the next step towards using them to compare AI-assisted work.
\end{abstract}

\section{What the observations determine}
\label{sec:introduction}

Suppose one person spends three hours working on a task during a four-hour interval. How much of that work occurred in the first two hours? Either half can contain at most two person-hours, so at least one person-hour must fall in the first half. The person could work one hour in the first half and two in the second, or two in the first and one in the second. Both limits are possible, giving a first-half labour range of one to two person-hours. Commit times leave a further ambiguity because the same sequence of commits can arise from different amounts of human activity.

\emph{Labour} is human time allocated to the work included in the study, measured in person-hours. In software work assisted by artificial intelligence (AI), it includes unsuccessful attempts and repair after acceptance as well as work leading to acceptance. To compare this labour with delivery, we also measure elapsed time to first acceptance and assess the accepted requirement a specified time later. For tasks accepted by the deadline, multiply the reference score by the fraction of that requirement still satisfied. This weighted score is zero for tasks that miss the acceptance deadline.

When several tasks share a person's time, their allocation fractions must sum to at most one across all tasks and types of work. This capacity constraint is the limit used in the opening example. Provided the observations and this constraint allow at least one allocation, our linear programme finds the smallest and largest possible labour totals.\footnote{A linear programme minimises or maximises a weighted sum subject to linear equalities and inequalities \cite[Section~4.3]{boydvandenberghe2004}. Here the unknowns are hours allocated within specified time intervals. The inequalities limit those hours using the observations and each person's available time.} Every total between them is also possible. Narrowing the range therefore requires further observations or justified restrictions on how people divided their time.

When exact labour totals are unavailable, we can estimate them by measuring labour in selected time intervals for individual people. Divide each person's period of work being studied into non-overlapping intervals and list them all. A probability sampling design selects from this list at random, giving every interval a known positive chance of selection. Predictions calculated for all listed intervals before selection give an initial total. We correct it using the selected intervals' differences between measured and predicted labour, each divided by that interval's selection probability. With accurate measurements of the sampled allocations, the corrected estimate averages to the true total over repeated samples. Predictions improve precision only when estimating their total error has lower sampling variance than estimating labour without them.\footnote{Sampling variance measures how widely estimates vary across the possible samples, as their probability-weighted mean squared distance from the average estimate. An unbiased estimator has an average equal to the true total. A particular sample can still give an estimate above or below that total.} Commit timestamps and code-change features can therefore aid labour estimation even when commits alone leave its exact value unresolved.

How do these two approaches describe uncertainty about total labour? The labour bounds identify exactly which totals are compatible with the observations and the rules for allocating each person's time. For the sample-based estimate, sampling uncertainty describes how the estimate would vary if the sampling procedure were repeated.

Interpreting a score derived from commit histories as person-hours requires calibration against labour measured separately from those histories. Standard Coder illustrates this requirement. Wright and Ziegler's method \cite{wright2019} infers coding activity from commit timing, then predicts those activity labels from code-change features. Its Standard Coding Hours can provide a common reference index. Calibration is needed because human activity and code changes can correspond to different amounts of labour, especially under AI assistance. This measurement question sits within broader accounts of productivity. Forsgren et al.\ distinguish activity and efficiency within their developer-productivity framework \cite{forsgren2021}, while Mozannar et al.\ examine the time costs of interacting with AI-assisted programming tools \cite{mozannar2024}.

Estimating AI's effect on labour or task delivery requires a causal study design that supports a comparison between outcomes with AI assistance and those that would arise without it. The chosen outcome determines what such a comparison establishes. Peng et al.\ examine completion time among completers of a programming exercise \cite{peng2023}. Becker et al.\ study experienced maintainers in familiar repositories, comparing log completion times after adjustment for forecast times \cite{becker2025}. Cui et al.\ study weekly completed pull requests in organisational experiments \cite{cui2026}, using allocation to study groups as an instrument for adoption in their June 2025 manuscript \cite[Section 4.1 and Table 3]{cui2025manuscript}. These studies use different populations and working conditions as well as different outcomes. Their estimates therefore answer different questions about AI-assisted work. A subsequent report from Model Evaluation and Threat Research describes selection and concurrent-agent measurement difficulties, supporting the need to measure human labour separately from elapsed time \cite{metr2026}. The calculations here use no data from these studies.

Sections~\ref{sec:observation} and~\ref{sec:accounting} explain why commits alone can leave person-hours unresolved, then define labour, acceptance time and weighted scores. Section~\ref{sec:sharp-identification} derives the full range of labour totals consistent with interval observations. Section~\ref{sec:estimation} turns to sampling and uses measured labour to correct predictions calculated before the sample is selected. Section~\ref{sec:study} sets out the measurement checks needed before comparing AI tools and working practices. The proofs apply standard optimisation and sampling results to the stated rules for allocating people's time. Appendix~\ref{app:scalar} gives a shortest-path solution for one person's work of one type on one task.

\section{Why commit histories can leave labour unresolved}
\label{sec:observation}

Why can the same commit distribution correspond to different amounts of activity? Commits in the model below depend on how often activity occurs and how often an active minute produces a commit. More frequent activity can be offset by a lower chance of a commit during each active minute.

To express this relationship, let $Z_t\in\{0,1\}$ be the hidden activity state in minute $t$, and let $C_t\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether a commit occurs in that minute. A fitted event model specifies how activity changes from minute to minute and how commits depend on that activity. Interpreting $Z_t=1$ as ``coding'' requires validation against labour measured separately from the commit history. The following example shows how these two probabilities can vary while the commit distribution stays the same. A Bernoulli variable takes the value one with its stated probability and zero otherwise.\footnote{In probability notation, $\Prb(A)$ is the probability of event $A$, and $\E[X]$ is the probability-weighted average of a random quantity $X$. The symbol $\sum_{t=1}^{n}$ means addition over minutes $1$ to $n$. The notation $Z_t\in\{0,1\}$ means that $Z_t$ is either zero or one. The interval $(0,1)$ contains values strictly between zero and one. Here $\pi$ denotes an activity probability.}

\begin{proposition}[Failure of unrestricted identification]
\label{prop:nonidentification}
Suppose the states $Z_t$ are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter $\pi\in(0,1)$, and, independently across minutes and of the states, $B_t$ are Bernoulli variables with parameter $c\in(0,1)$. Define $C_t=Z_tB_t$. The joint distribution of all commit observations depends on $(\pi,c)$ only through $\pi c$. In particular, $(\pi,c)=(1/2,1/5)$ and $(1/4,2/5)$ give identical commit distributions but different expected active times.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each $C_t$ is Bernoulli with parameter $\theta=\pi c$, and the $C_t$ are independent. For every $n\geq1$ and every binary string $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:event-law}
 \Prb(C_1=e_1,\ldots,C_n=e_n)
 =\theta^{\sum_t e_t}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum_t e_t}.
\end{equation}
Both parameter pairs give $\theta=1/10$. In contrast,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:activity-total}
 \E\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_t\right]=n\pi,
\end{equation}
which is $n/2$ minutes in the first model and $n/4$ in the second. Thus identical commit distributions can correspond to different expected numbers of active minutes.
\end{proof}

In Equation~\eqref{eq:event-law}, $e_t$ specifies whether minute $t$ contains a commit, and $\sum_t e_t$ counts those minutes across the specified sequence. Equation~\eqref{eq:activity-total} instead gives the expected number of hidden active minutes. Both models assign the same probability to every commit sequence while giving different expected active times. This is why the commit distribution alone cannot distinguish the two models.

This example is a special case of a two-state hidden Markov model with independent activity states, in which the next activity state has the same distribution for either current state. It shows that this model family allows different activity levels to produce the same commit distribution. Generic-identifiability results for finite-state hidden Markov models concern parameters outside exceptional sets and permit hidden-state relabelling \cite[Section~6.1]{allman2009}.\footnote{In a hidden Markov model, the next hidden state depends on the current state, and observations are conditionally independent given the state sequence, each depending on its corresponding state. Generic identifiability means that the observed distribution determines the parameters, up to state relabelling, outside an exceptional set of parameter values. Relabelling changes the names of states and their associated parameters together. The example above lies in the independent-state subfamily.} To identify the parameters, state which model restrictions or additional measurements distinguish the possible models. Restrictions on the activity model, informative code-change features or separately obtained measurements of activity may supply that distinction.

Even after the activity model is identified, interpreting its states as human labour requires validation against person-hours measured separately from the commit history used to fit it. Under AI assistance, commits can continue while human attention is elsewhere, so the relationship between activity and commits may change. Measured interval totals instead constrain how much labour occurred during specified periods. Using those measurements requires a definition of which work belongs to each task and when that work is counted.

\section{Counting labour and assessing task outcomes}
\label{sec:accounting}

\subsection{Defining tasks and allocating human time}

Identify each task by the change requested and the criteria for accepting it. A GitHub issue, for example, can describe the requested behaviour and the checks a reviewer will use to accept the implementation. Its commits and pull requests can change while the issue continues to identify the same requested work. The study protocol states which tasks are eligible for inclusion. Begin tracking task $i$ at time $s_i$, when its requirement, acceptance criteria and eligibility under those rules have been documented. Keep the requirement version used at that time, for example by saving the issue description when tracking begins.

Requirements can later change, and tasks can be split or merged. Define how these changes will enter the analysis before inspecting labour totals, acceptance times or weighted scores. In the GitHub example, dated issue edits show which requirement or acceptance criterion changed. Links between issues identify the original task and any components or merged tasks. Decide whether accepted components receive their own scores or contribute only through the completed parent, counting each contribution once. If one component is accepted before the deadline and another afterwards, counting components recognises the first in time, while counting the parent waits for both. Regrouping can therefore change the weighted total even when the component reference scores, defined below, sum to the parent's score.

AI access is part of the working conditions being compared. The study plan must specify which tools and model versions each group may use, which tasks they may assist and which autonomous actions are permitted. It must also identify the required human review and release approval, including who is responsible for those checks. Record actual tool use separately from these permissions. For example, a task diary can identify the AI tool used and the work it assisted. Access and use can differ because difficult tasks may prompt more use, while people may decline an available tool. Comparing groups by use alone can therefore mix the effect of access with differences in task difficulty.

How should human time be counted when several AI workflows run at once? Count the time each person spends on the work and calculate automated execution time separately. Let $a_{ijr}(t)$ be the fraction of person $j$'s time spent on task $i$ doing work of type $r$, such as implementation or review, at calendar time $t$. Each person's combined allocation is at most one. Integrating an allocation fraction over time gives the person's labour on that task and type of work. Adding these contributions gives the task's total labour. With finite sets of tasks, people and types of work, and measurable allocation functions,\footnote{The integral $\int_s^e a(t)\,dt$ accumulates the allocation fraction over elapsed time. A constant allocation $a$ over $e-s$ hours contributes $a(e-s)$ person-hours. These are Lebesgue integrals with respect to time. Measurability ensures that sets defined by allocation thresholds have a well-defined duration. The phrase ``almost everywhere'' used below permits exceptions on a set of total duration zero, such as individual switching instants. Such exceptions leave these integrals unchanged.} labour through task age $u\geq0$, measured from the start of tracking, is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:labour}
 a_{ijr}(t)\geq0,\qquad \sum_{i,r}a_{ijr}(t)\leq1,\qquad
 H_i(u)=\sum_{j,r}\int_{s_i}^{s_i+u}a_{ijr}(t)\,dt.
\end{equation}
With time in hours, $H_i(u)$ has units of person-hours. Two people working for an hour can therefore contribute two person-hours. The allocation constraint limits each person's combined contribution during that hour, including when several sessions run concurrently.

A person may switch between prompting one workflow and reviewing or integrating another's results while both workflows continue to run. The allocation functions represent the fraction of the person's time spent on each activity, including supervision. Dividing that time between tasks keeps the combined allocation within the person's available time, so each period of human work is counted once.

For example, consider one person using two AI workflows during a one-hour interval. The person prepares workflow A's prompt during minutes 0--10 and submits it at minute 10. Workflow A starts then and finishes at minute 60. The person prepares and submits workflow B's prompt during minutes 10--20. Workflow B executes from minute 20 to minute 40, after which the person reviews its result until minute 60. Assume no other human work on these tasks during the interval. Human labour totals forty minutes, or $2/3$ of a person-hour. Adding the two execution durations gives seventy minutes, or $7/6$ workflow-hours. Figure~\ref{fig:human-and-workflow-time} shows how both totals arise.

To apply this labour account, decide which activities count and how shared work is assigned to tasks. Include the work from specification and implementation through review, testing and repair, and state whether attended waiting counts. Shared review and platform work need an attribution rule so their person-hours can enter Equation~\eqref{eq:labour}. List measured person-hours that cannot yet be assigned to a task separately from task totals. An unknown task assignment leaves the work's attribution unresolved even when its duration is known.

Count automated execution separately from human labour, including when a person attends to a running workflow. In the example, a workflow-hour is one hour of elapsed execution for one workflow. Add the durations of the individual runs within the observation period, so overlapping runs each contribute their own duration. State which start and stop events delimit a run. CPU usage, token use and charges require separate measurements with their own units. Combining human time with computing resources requires a stated conversion into costs. The combined measure then describes cost rather than labour.

Elapsed time to acceptance includes periods when the task is queued or its workflows run without human attention. Let $T_i$ be elapsed time from the start of tracking to first acceptance. Starting at $s_i$ counts queueing and any reasoning or specification between $s_i$ and the first commit. If the task is permanently abandoned before acceptance, put $T_i=\infty$. If the study loses track of an unfinished task, its eventual acceptance time is unknown and is treated as censored. Acceptance and deployment may occur at different times. Keep their timestamps separately, for example an acceptance confirmation in the task issue and a deployment timestamp in a release log.

\subsection{Scoring tasks and assessing requirement fulfilment}

Assign each task a nonnegative reference score $S_i^0$ under a common scoring rule. The score may count tasks or functional units, or use Standard Coding Hours as weights. Keep each value with the scoring-rule version and the inputs used to calculate it. For example, a task's scoring entry could identify its GitHub issue and requirement version, then give the assigned reference score. The superscript $0$ denotes the common reference. Keep each task's reference score unchanged across the comparisons, using the same scoring rule and weights for both groups.

The information used to score a task determines whether AI can change the reference score itself. For a causal comparison of fixed requirements, prefer the requirement version saved when tracking begins. A score based on final code depends on the implementation, which AI may change, so reporting such a score requires making that dependence explicit. Splitting or squashing a code change can also alter a nonlinear score. To keep the reference consistent, score the same task information once or use a requirement-scoring rule whose component scores sum to the total. Count the delivered requirement once across retries, failed attempts and reverts.

A score expressed in person-hours needs calibration against labour measured separately from the commit histories used by the predictor. Specify whose labour is included, which types of work count and which quality requirements apply. Also state the last task age included in the labour total. A predictor trained on activity states inferred from commits learns to predict those states. Interpreting its predictions as person-hours requires comparison with separately measured human time under these inclusion rules and the same task-age limit.

How does the later requirement assessment enter the calculation? For a task accepted by the deadline, multiply its reference score by the fraction of its accepted requirement still satisfied at that assessment. A task that misses the deadline contributes a weighted score of zero. Choose an acceptance deadline at task age $\tau>0$ and an assessment delay $\Delta\geq0$ measured from first acceptance. This later check is the follow-up assessment. Specify how reviewers will judge partial fulfilment of the accepted requirement. For accepted tasks, let $R_i(\Delta)\in[0,1]$ be the fraction still satisfied at time $s_i+T_i+\Delta$, judged using these criteria. The task's weighted score is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
 Q_i(\tau,\Delta)=\begin{cases} S_i^0 R_i(\Delta),&T_i\leq\tau,\\ 0,&T_i>\tau.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The weighted score has the same reference unit as the initial score. For example, suppose a task has an initial reference score of ten units and is accepted before the deadline. An assessment rule might give equal weight to ten acceptance criteria. If eight are still met at the later assessment, the assessed fraction is 0.8 and the weighted score is eight reference units. This is one possible rule for partial fulfilment. The study must choose a rule suited to its requirements and explain how intentional replacement is distinguished from loss caused by a defect. Lines of code that remain in place, tests that pass and an absence of reported incidents each give only partial evidence that the requirement is still satisfied. The measurement pilot must check which defects the assessment detects and how closely independent reviewers agree.

Dividing the sum of weighted scores by total human labour gives the productivity ratio, measured in reference units per person-hour. For a specified finite set of tasks with positive total labour, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cohort-productivity}
 P_H(\tau,\Delta)
 =\frac{\sum_i Q_i(\tau,\Delta)}{\sum_i H_i(\tau+\Delta)}.
\end{equation}
The denominator counts labour through the common task age $\tau+\Delta$ for every task in that set. It therefore includes labour on rejected, unfinished and abandoned tasks, together with work after acceptance that is assigned to the task. Within this common period, an early acceptance leaves more time for repair than a late acceptance. Counting repair only until $\Delta$ after each acceptance would make the end of labour counting depend on acceptance time and define a different denominator. The ratio used here includes all labour assigned to each task through task age $\tau+\Delta$.

An individual task can contribute a positive weighted score with zero human labour. The ratio requires positive labour only across the task set as a whole. Report both totals alongside the ratio so readers can see which quantity drives any difference.

\section{Estimating labour from sampled allocations}
\label{sec:estimation}

The preceding bounds describe every labour total permitted by a given set of interval observations. What changes when labour is measured only in randomly selected intervals for individual people? We estimate total labour and assess how the estimate varies across the possible samples. Treating that estimate as an exact interval total can exclude the true allocation through sampling error, so its uncertainty needs separate analysis. The difference estimator below corrects a predicted total using measured labour from the selected intervals. Under its sampling and measurement assumptions, the corrected estimate averages to the true total over repeated samples.

The sampling frame is the complete list of time intervals for individual people from which the sample is selected. It must cover all work included in the labour total, including reasoning away from the keyboard as well as review and coordination. For a selected interval, a short work diary can identify the person and time period, then give the minutes spent on each task and type of work. These durations supply the labour measurements used by the estimator. Check a subset using a separately obtained account of the same intervals. For example, an observer could time activities and compare those durations with the participant's diary. Explain who made each measurement and how differences were investigated, and report any disagreement that remains. Missing diary entries leave selected intervals unmeasured, while inaccurate recall can distort the durations supplied. Work omitted from the sampling frame requires a separate check of whether its list covers the intended labour total.

\section{Validating the measurements}
\label{sec:study}

Applying these calculations to workplace labour requires measurements that cover the included activities and accurately describe each person's time on them. A measurement pilot must test this coverage and accuracy before the measures are used to compare AI tools. Define the included work and the rules for assigning hours to tasks, then list the time intervals for individual people available for sampling. Before collecting labour measurements or assessing tasks, specify the reference scoring rule, acceptance deadline and criteria for judging requirement fulfilment. Together these choices determine which human work is counted and how delivered requirements are scored.

The pilot must show how often selected intervals receive usable labour measurements and how much time participants and researchers spend supplying and checking them. Include periods with concurrent AI workflows when checking how shared oversight and attended waiting are assigned under the inclusion rules. Report missing diary entries and disagreements about time allocations. Check how many eligible tasks receive their requirement assessment at the scheduled time, and measure the time needed to complete this follow-up. Summarise these measurement checks by comparison group, type of work and defined task category. They determine the collection effort and precision that a later comparison can support.

The validation checks must match the result we intend to use. Labour bounds need independently checked interval totals and justified limits on measurement error. Choose the periods for which labour will be bounded before inspecting the study's labour totals, acceptance times or weighted scores. For each period, compare the sum of separately calculated bounds with the minimum and maximum from the joint calculation. This shows how much accounting for each person's shared time narrows the labour range.

For sampled labour, assess whether predictions improve precision by comparing variance with and without them under the same sampling design. Calculate predictions before sampling and keep them unchanged whichever sample is drawn. Retain the selection probabilities and the information about joint selection needed to calculate uncertainty. The next subsection explains how to account for this sampling variation when assessing prediction accuracy.

\subsection{Conditions for comparing AI tools and working practices}

Accurate labour measurements and requirement assessments allow groups to be compared. When can a difference be attributed to AI access? The groups must support a comparison that separates the effect of AI access from other differences in their work and working conditions. AI adoption can change which work is requested or undertaken, so the study must first define which work enters the comparison. A study of tasks specified before group assignment answers a different question from a study of incoming organisational work.

For tasks specified in advance, decide which qualify before assigning tasks, people or teams to groups, and keep those inclusion rules unchanged. State how earlier work is counted. In the request list, show the status of postponed, rejected and abandoned requests and identify which belong to the included task set. An organisation-wide study instead needs a common calendar period, a list of requests received during that period and the tasks already in progress at its start. Each design must identify the population it concerns and explain how measurements are weighted to represent it.

The comparison groups must reflect how teams share work. Reviewers and reusable components can carry the effects of one team's AI access into another team's work. Shared computing resources can also link outcomes when teams compete for capacity. Account for these links when forming groups and analysing outcomes. Analyse teams according to their original group assignment and log actual tool use separately.

Calculate uncertainty in the estimated effect of AI access using the units assigned to groups, such as teams or individual people. More commits within a team increase the number of commit events but leave the number of independently assigned teams unchanged. The uncertainty calculation must also include the effects of measuring labour and checking requirement fulfilment in subsets.

Apply the same criteria for requirement fulfilment and the same follow-up assessment procedure to both groups. An assessment note can identify the task and requirement version checked, the assessment time and the reviewer's judgement. Have two reviewers assess a subset independently so their disagreement can be measured. Reviews can prompt repair, so include the time feedback reaches the team and whether the reviewer knew the task's AI-group assignment in the assessment note. Count any resulting repair within the period used to calculate labour for the productivity ratio. Estimating the sum of weighted scores from assessments of a subset of tasks requires known selection probabilities and assumptions about the review's effect on later work. Report severe defects separately because the fraction of a requirement still satisfied gives only part of the evidence about security and reliability.

Interpret any apparent labour saving alongside acceptance and weighted scores. Follow every task through $\tau+\Delta$ as required by Section~\ref{sec:accounting}, distinguishing permanent abandonment from losing contact with a team or ceasing to track a task. When labour measurements, acceptance times or later requirement assessments are missing, state the assumptions or bounds used for those unknown quantities. Reduced labour may reflect more abandonment, so report task acceptance and both score and labour totals. The productivity ratio also needs uncertainty in its numerator and denominator, including how they vary together.

Before inspecting the study's labour totals, acceptance times or weighted scores, decide what improvement would be useful and what loss of requirement fulfilment would be acceptable. Set the improvement thresholds and the maximum acceptable quality reduction, called the noninferiority margin. To show that a loss falls within this margin, estimate its size and uncertainty. An undetected reduction may still exceed the margin. The following fixed-workload experiment makes both decision criteria explicit.

\section{Results and next measurement checks}
\label{sec:limits}

Different distributions of human activity can produce identical commit distributions. Interval totals, or justified bounds on them, restrict the labour possible in a specified period when combined with each person's available time. Dividing time at the start and end of every observed interval and every period whose labour total is sought turns these restrictions into a finite optimisation problem with exactly the same possible labour totals. Accounting jointly for tasks that share a person's time can exclude totals allowed by adding separate bounds. The summed lower bound is attainable only if all component minima can occur together. The same requirement applies to the upper bound and component maxima.

Selecting intervals at random gives an unbiased labour estimate when the sampling frame covers the work, selection probabilities are known and positive, and sampled allocations are measured accurately. Predictions calculated before selection and held unchanged across possible samples improve precision only when estimating their total error has lower sampling variance than estimating labour without predictions. Applying either result requires evidence that the measured durations cover the intended work and assign each person's time accurately. For example, a work diary must include reasoning and coordination as well as implementation. The measurement pilot must test that coverage and compare reported durations with separately obtained time measurements.

Establishing an AI benefit requires a causal comparison of tools and working practices. An organisation-wide adoption study would include changes to incoming requirements, whereas the proposed experiment concerns a fixed workload. Its next empirical step is the measurement pilot in Section~\ref{sec:causal-experiment}. The pilot must test whether work diaries or other time measurements cover the included activities, and whether reviewers can assess accepted requirements at the scheduled follow-up times. Disagreement about measured time or requirement fulfilment, missing measurements and collection effort will guide the measurement procedure and the size of the main experiment.
