\paragraph{A labour forecast at intake.}
\label{par:labour-reference}
Consider a team planning the person-hours it will spend on a new task through age $u=\tau+\Delta$. A simple benchmark uses the mean labour of earlier tasks in the same intake class. Before evaluating any forecasts, specify the classes using information available at intake, such as the type of request and the size of its initial requirement. Fix the rules for the history window and for broadening a class with too few observations.

At intake time $c$, form $D_c(x)$ from earlier tasks in class $x$ whose full $u$-hour follow-up and labour records were available by $c$, including unfinished and abandoned tasks. Let $h_j=H_j(u)$ denote independently ascertained person-hours under the same accounting rules. When the chosen class has enough observations, the forecast for a task in that class is
\[
 g_c(x)=\frac{1}{|D_c(x)|}\sum_{j\in D_c(x)}h_j .
\]
A verified zero-hour task contributes zero to the sum and one to the count. For example, historical totals of $0$, $2$ and $4$ person-hours give a mean of $2$. Missing labour leaves the class mean for all eligible tasks unresolved. Identify whose records are missing and justify any adjustment or restrict the forecast to the population the records cover. A poorly represented class uses the wider group specified in advance if that group is comparable. If neither group qualifies, record that a forecast could not be issued.

This use of historical labour assumes the earlier tasks still represent incoming work. Team composition, tool access and the way engineers report hours can all change that relation. Test each forecast on later intakes using only information available when it was issued. Keep all versions of a requirement together and exclude historical tasks whose labour window was still open at that point. Report the number of historical tasks and the teams and period they represent. Show signed errors before and after a change in staffing or tools, since a model fitted to the previous workload can systematically understate or overstate current labour. Compare the benchmark and its competitor on tasks for which both issued forecasts. Give the fraction of all eligible intakes each could forecast.

\paragraph{Sampling error in validation.}
A four-hour forecast can disagree with sampled labour even when the engineers spent exactly four hours on the task. Hold the task-level benchmark and its competitor fixed before drawing the validation sample. These forecasts predict the labour of a task from information available at intake. Equation~\eqref{eq:difference-estimator} serves a different purpose. It corrects a frame total after selected intervals have been observed, using a prediction for every interval in that frame.

Treat each task's labour $H$ in a finite validation population as fixed. Let $f$ be its prediction, calculated before validation sampling and unchanged whichever sample is drawn, and suppose $\E_{\!s}[\widehat H]=H$ with finite design variance. Then
\begin{align*}
 \E_{\!s}[f-\widehat H]&=f-H,\\
 \E_{\!s}[(f-\widehat H)^2]&=(f-H)^2+\Var_{\!s}(\widehat H).
\end{align*}
Expanding the squared difference proves the second identity because the cross term has expectation zero when $\E_{\!s}[\widehat H-H]=0$. The signed difference therefore measures prediction error on average. Squaring the difference also counts sampling variance. These identities hold for each task under the sampling design used to select its observed intervals. Uncertainty in combined results also depends on how the tasks' estimates vary together.

For a second prediction $g$, such as the task-class mean at intake, held fixed across the same validation samples, subtracting the expected squared errors cancels the common sampling variance, giving
\[
 \E_{\!s}[(f-\widehat H)^2-(g-\widehat H)^2]
 =(f-H)^2-(g-H)^2.
\]
The same sample can therefore compare two predictions without attributing its sampling variance to either model. For one task the observed difference equals $(f-g)(f+g-2\widehat H)$, with design variance $4(f-g)^2\Var_s(\widehat H)$. For the absolute level of squared error, $(f-\widehat H)^2-\widehat V$ is unbiased for $(f-H)^2$ whenever $\widehat V$ is unbiased for $\Var_s(\widehat H)$. It can be negative on an individual sample. When one sampled shift contributes to several tasks, use the joint sampling design to quantify uncertainty in the mean score difference across tasks.

Absolute error and interval coverage need a separate check because the squared-error calculation does not justify them. To examine these measures, consider eight one-hour person-intervals with allocations $(1,1,1,1,0,0,0,0)$. Sample one uniformly, with inclusion probability $1/8$. True labour is $H=4$, whereas its Horvitz--Thompson estimate is $0$ or $8$, each with probability $1/2$. A perfect prediction $f=4$ therefore differs from either sampled estimate by $4$. The interval $[3.5,4.5]$ always contains true labour but never contains the sampled estimate. Here the discrepancy comes entirely from sampling error in the labour estimate. The signed difference between prediction and sampled estimate remains unbiased for the prediction's error against true labour. Absolute error and interval coverage calculated against $\widehat H$ measure agreement with the sampled estimate, including its sampling error.

Prediction validation must distinguish prediction error from uncertainty in the labour measurements. One option is to select tasks for validation and record every person-interval in their target windows. Check those records independently, stating who reviewed them, which evidence resolved discrepancies and how the review was independent of the people who supplied the observations. Report any remaining measurement error. Alternatively, use a stated measurement model under which the observations uniquely determine the chosen accuracy measure. Retain selection probabilities and joint sampling information for uncertainty calculations. Report systematic overstatement or understatement and individual prediction errors by study group, type of work and defined task category. Correlation between group averages must be supplemented by these checks.

Validate the output scores as well, so prediction accuracy can be assessed alongside the work delivered. Give two reviewers the same requirement and follow-up evidence packaged as one pull request and as three. Hold the acceptance date and delivered behaviour fixed. A change in task-level score then exposes sensitivity to packaging. As a second check, move one component past the deadline and verify that both reviewers apply the agreed rule for counting accepted components. Report reviewer disagreement and follow-up completeness alongside prediction error. Recalculate group comparisons using plausible amounts of missing labour and disputed output. If those measurement errors change which group performs better, choosing between tools and working practices requires resolving or explicitly accepting that uncertainty.
