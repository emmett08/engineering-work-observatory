\paragraph{A labour forecast at intake.}
\label{par:labour-reference}
When a new task arrives, a team can use earlier tasks of the same kind to forecast how many person-hours it will need through task age $u=\tau+\Delta$. For a simple benchmark, define the task classes before evaluation. Choose characteristics known at intake, such as request type or the size of the initial requirement. Specify how far back the team will look, how many earlier tasks a class must contain and which broader class it may use when that minimum is unmet.

For a task arriving at time $c$ in class $x$, let $D_c(x)$ contain earlier tasks of that class whose full labour window had closed by $c$ and whose hours were known then. Keep eligible tasks that the team abandoned or left unfinished. For each historical task $j$, let $h_j=H_j(u)$ be the person-hours independently measured under the same accounting rules. If the class meets the minimum, forecast its mean labour
\[
 g_c(x)=\frac{1}{|D_c(x)|}\sum_{j\in D_c(x)}h_j .
\]
The mean of tasks taking $0$, $2$ and $4$ person-hours is $2$ person-hours. The zero-hour task counts because someone verified its labour. If hours are missing, identify the affected tasks. A forecast for all eligible tasks then needs an assumption about the missing hours. Explain any adjustment or restrict the forecast to the population for which hours are available. Use the prespecified broader class only when its tasks are comparable and its history meets the minimum. If it also fails to qualify, mark the forecast unavailable.

The benchmark assumes that earlier tasks still represent incoming work. New staffing or tools may change the time needed for a task. Even if that time stays the same, a change in how engineers report their hours may shift the historical mean. Test the benchmark on later intakes. At each intake, use only information then available, keeping all versions of a requirement on the same side of the training and evaluation split. Report how many historical tasks qualified, which teams handled them and when. Examine signed errors before and after a change in staffing or tools to see whether the forecasts systematically miss in one direction. Compare competing forecasts on the same tasks. For each method, report the proportion of eligible intakes for which it issued a forecast.

We checked the benchmark on a synthetic team with five historical requirements and eight later intakes. Each forecast used only hours available at intake and required at least two earlier measurements. The class mean supplied seven forecasts. It had no earlier hours for the team's first migration. Pooling all task types supplied eight. On the seven tasks both methods could forecast, their mean absolute errors against known labour were $1.19$ person-hours for the class mean and $2.48$ for the pooled mean. The class mean still underestimated two later fixes that took six hours each. These results show how the chosen history affects forecast availability and error. Testing the same comparison in a workplace requires independent labour measurements for later intakes.

\paragraph{Sampling error in validation.}
A team forecasts four hours for a task. Its labour estimate from a random sample of intervals can differ from four hours even when the forecast is exactly right. The four-hour forecast concerns a task whose work has yet to begin. By the time we judge its accuracy, we can use the difference estimator in Equation~\eqref{eq:difference-estimator} to estimate the hours worked from a sample of intervals.

For a task with fixed labour $H$ in the finite validation population, calculate its forecast $f$ before drawing the validation sample. Keep $f$ fixed across possible samples. Suppose the labour estimate satisfies $\E_{\!s}[\widehat H]=H$ and has finite design variance. Then
\begin{align*}
 \E_{\!s}[f-\widehat H]&=f-H,\\
 \E_{\!s}[(f-\widehat H)^2]&=(f-H)^2+\Var_{\!s}(\widehat H).
\end{align*}
To obtain the second identity, expand the square and use $\E_{\!s}[\widehat H-H]=0$ to cancel the cross term. The expected signed error equals the forecast's error against true labour. The expected squared error includes the sampling variance.

Let $g$ be a second forecast for the same task, such as its class mean at intake. Keep both forecasts fixed across validation samples and use the same labour estimate to score them. Their expected difference in squared errors is
\[
 \E_{\!s}[(f-\widehat H)^2-(g-\widehat H)^2]
 =(f-H)^2-(g-H)^2.
\]
Using the same estimate cancels the common variance term in expectation. For a single task, the observed difference is $(f-g)(f+g-2\widehat H)$, with design variance $4(f-g)^2\Var_s(\widehat H)$. A shift that contributes hours to several tasks can make their score differences vary together. Include those covariances when estimating uncertainty in the average score difference. To estimate the squared error of one forecast against true labour, subtract an unbiased estimate $\widehat V$ of $\Var_s(\widehat H)$ from $(f-\widehat H)^2$. This correction is unbiased for $(f-H)^2$, although its value on a particular sample can be negative.

Systematic errors in the measured hours can reverse the ranking. If $b=\E_s[\widehat H]-H$, the expected difference in squared errors shifts by $-2(f-g)b$ relative to the comparison with true labour. Suppose a task takes four hours and both forecasts are scored against a reference that consistently misses one hour of review. A three-hour forecast then appears exact and the correct four-hour forecast appears one hour too high. When the audit supplies a range for $b$, use its endpoints to check whether the preferred forecast changes.

Absolute error and interval coverage require further evidence. Suppose a task occupies four of eight one-hour intervals in full, with allocations $(1,1,1,1,0,0,0,0)$. With one interval drawn uniformly, each has selection probability $1/8$. True labour is $H=4$ hours. Its Horvitz--Thompson estimate takes the values $0$ and $8$ with equal probability. The forecast $f=4$ then has absolute error zero against true labour and absolute error four against either sampled estimate. The interval $[3.5,4.5]$ contains the true value. Its chance of containing the sampled estimate is zero. Scoring against $\widehat H$ therefore measures agreement with a quantity that varies with the selected interval.

On a probability sample of validation tasks, check every contributor's eligible intervals against evidence collected independently of the forecasts. Where that evidence leaves hours uncertain, report the range of forecast errors it permits. Another approach is to specify a measurement model and show that its observations determine the chosen accuracy measure. Retain the selection probabilities needed to quantify uncertainty, including those for pairs of intervals where required. Report errors for individual tasks and examine their direction by type of work and task class.

The cohort calculation uses a requirement's initial score and the fraction of it still satisfied at follow-up. In a controlled example, a requirement has four equally weighted criteria and takes one person-hour. Three criteria still hold. The calculation gives three reference units per person-hour whether the change appears in one pull request or three. If the assessment credits all four criteria in error, the rate rises to four units per person-hour for identical delivered behaviour.

To find out whether reviewers themselves change the score when work is split, prepare the same two versions with identical behaviour and acceptance dates. Have each of two reviewers score both versions, reversing their order for the second reviewer. Compare each reviewer's two scores. Next, move one component past the deadline to test whether both reviewers apply the agreed counting rule. Report their disagreements and the proportion of tasks assessed at follow-up. Repeat the group comparison across plausible values for missing hours and disputed scores. If the preferred practice changes, identify the observation that could resolve the choice.
