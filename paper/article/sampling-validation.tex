\paragraph{A labour forecast at intake.}
\label{par:labour-reference}
At intake, the forecast targets cumulative person-hours through requirement age $u=\tau+\Delta$. A benchmark uses earlier requirements of the same type or initial size, both observable at intake. Specify the lookback period and minimum number of measurements for each class before evaluation. A broader class of comparable requirements can supply a fallback.

For a task entering class $x$ at time $c$, let $D_c(x)$ contain earlier eligible tasks in that class whose labour windows had closed and whose measured hours were available by $c$. Eligibility includes tasks the team discontinued or had yet to finish when the window closed. Each contributes $h_j=H_j(u)$ person-hours, measured independently under the same accounting rules. Once the class meets the minimum, its forecast is
\[
 g_c(x)=\frac{1}{|D_c(x)|}\sum_{j\in D_c(x)}h_j .
\]
Three verified totals of $0$, $2$ and $4$ person-hours, for example, give a forecast of $2$. If eligible tasks lack measurements, identify the omissions. Explain any adjustment for missing hours and the assumption that supports it, or limit conclusions to tasks with measurements. If the original class falls short of the minimum, use the prespecified fallback only if it meets the same minimum. Withhold the forecast if both classes fall short.

Historical hours predict incoming work to the extent that earlier requirements remain representative. New tools may shorten review. A change in timekeeping may lower measured review hours even when reviews take as long as before. A prospective evaluation dates each forecast at intake, uses information then available and keeps all versions of a requirement together when separating training from evaluation. For each forecast, identify the teams, dates and number of tasks contributing to its history. Average signed errors before and after a change in staffing or tools can expose persistent bias. Compare forecasts on common tasks and give the proportion of all eligible intakes each method covered.

At one synthetic team, five requirements supplied the initial history for eight subsequent intakes. The benchmark required at least two earlier measurements. The class mean covered seven intakes, omitting the team's first migration. Pooling all task types covered eight. On the seven common tasks, mean absolute error was $1.19$ person-hours for the class mean and $2.48$ for the pooled mean. Two later bug fixes each took six hours, far above their class forecasts of two hours and $8/3$ hours respectively. Testing these methods on future intakes requires independent labour measurements.

\paragraph{Sampling error in validation.}
A correct four-hour forecast can appear wrong if a sample of the team's time gives an estimate other than four hours.

For a task with fixed labour $H$, let $f$ be its intake forecast and $\widehat H$ its labour estimated later, for example by \eqref{eq:difference-estimator}. Keeping $f$ fixed over the validation draws, suppose $\E_{\!s}[\widehat H]=H$ and the design variance is finite. Then
\begin{align*}
 \E_{\!s}[f-\widehat H]&=f-H,\\
 \E_{\!s}[(f-\widehat H)^2]&=(f-H)^2+\Var_{\!s}(\widehat H).
\end{align*}
The cross term in the square vanishes because $\E_{\!s}[\widehat H-H]=0$. Sampling preserves signed error in expectation and inflates expected squared error by the design variance.

Compare $f$ with a second intake forecast $g$, such as the historical class mean. When both forecasts are fixed across draws and scored against the same labour estimate, the variance term cancels in expectation,
\[
 \E_{\!s}[(f-\widehat H)^2-(g-\widehat H)^2]
 =(f-H)^2-(g-H)^2.
\]
For one task the observed difference is $(f-g)(f+g-2\widehat H)$, with variance $4(f-g)^2\Var_s(\widehat H)$. When tasks share a sampled shift, the variance of their average difference includes the covariance between their differences. For an individual forecast, subtracting an unbiased estimate $\widehat V$ of $\Var_s(\widehat H)$ from $(f-\widehat H)^2$ yields an unbiased estimate of $(f-H)^2$. On a particular draw the corrected value may be negative.

Bias in the reference hours can reverse a forecast ranking. If $b=\E_s[\widehat H]-H$, the expected difference in squared errors shifts by $-2(f-g)b$. Omitting an hour of review from a four-hour task makes a three-hour forecast appear exact and the correct forecast an hour too high. The shift is linear in $b$, so evaluating both endpoints of an audit range for $b$ shows whether bias within that range could reverse the ranking.

A task that fully occupies four of eight one-hour intervals has allocations $(1,1,1,1,0,0,0,0)$ and true labour $H=4$ hours. One uniformly selected interval, with inclusion probability $1/8$, gives a Horvitz--Thompson estimate of $0$ or $8$ hours with equal probability. Either reference gives the exact four-hour forecast an apparent absolute error of four hours. The prediction interval $[3.5,4.5]$ contains the true labour but excludes both possible estimates. Scoring coverage against the sampled hours therefore gives zero.

To assess absolute error or coverage, draw a probability sample of validation tasks and check each contributor's eligible intervals against independent evidence. Where the evidence gives only a range for labour, propagate that range to forecast error. A measurement model can identify accuracy if every measurement process consistent with the observations gives the same value. The sampling uncertainty depends on the selection probabilities, including pair probabilities where required by the design. Examining signed errors by task type can expose systematic overprediction or underprediction.
