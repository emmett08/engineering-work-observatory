# Where stronger measurements or weaker assumptions change the result

This note examines the conditions that affect the conclusions in the longer paper. The time-capacity calculation gives an enclosure even when the workplace restricts interruptions. An explicit allowance for undocumented work protects that enclosure when there is evidence for an overall cap. A paired trial can reach a decision from incomplete outcomes if prespecified ranges and randomisation uncertainty together leave sufficient evidence.

## Identifying labour

| Condition | Change and remaining requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Commit times identify work only under additional restrictions | Use brief independent observations of human activity, including times with no commit, to distinguish commit propensity from time spent working. In the paper's deliberately simple model, the commit rate is the activity rate multiplied by the conditional commit probability. An exact positive value for either factor determines the other. A sufficiently representative activity sample estimates the total directly and makes that model unnecessary for this purpose. Automatic commits and changes in work mix call for measurements that cover both work with a commit and work without one. |
| The total refers to a particular group of people and work | Include an unresolved-hours variable in each plausible person's time budget and allocate it among eligible work, other work and idle time as the evidence permits. Allow an additional group with a separate upper limit when an audit supports that limit for unlisted contributors. With no evidence limiting their number or available time, even a perfectly measured team total gives no finite upper limit for all contributors. |
| Exact allocation assumes freedom to switch activities within each calendar cell | Read the original minimum and maximum as outer limits for every more restrictive schedule that satisfies the same observed totals and time budgets. The endpoints may cease to be attainable, and feasible totals can have gaps. If one hour of work in a two-hour period must run entirely before or entirely after the midpoint, the first-hour totals are zero and one. The enclosing interval remains valid, although a half hour cannot occur. For restrictions that depend only on the simultaneous activities allowed within a cell, assign a nonnegative duration to each permitted combination and require the durations to sum to the cell length. Each allocation gives such durations, and placing the combinations in successive blocks gives the converse when combinations may switch freely. Minimum block lengths and dependencies across cells require separate scheduling constraints. |
| The measured totals and time limits contain the truth | Carry known rounding or audit tolerances into the constraints. When several input limits come from a probability sample, allocate a total error probability across them. The union bound then guarantees simultaneous coverage at the chosen level without assuming independent measurement errors. The guarantee still depends on defensible limits for bias and on coverage of the actual work. An infeasible problem means the supplied observations and restrictions conflict. |
| The retained score depends on which requirements were accepted and still work | Freeze the cohort and initial scoring rules before treatment. For a missing follow-up on a requirement worth $w$ initial units, carry its possible retained score as $[0,w]$. A verified failure of one criterion or an independently confirmed late acceptance can tighten this range. Treat permanent discontinuation as zero under the stated endpoint. A scoring rule is a choice about which behaviour matters. A mathematical model cannot choose it on the team’s behalf. |

## Sampling and comparing rates

| Condition | Change and remaining requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Every eligible interval has a known positive probability of selection | Publish the draw rule and check its coverage against the people and periods in the target. Treat work with zero selection probability as a separate unknown contribution. Two work histories that agree on every selectable interval can differ arbitrarily within the feasible limits of the excluded intervals. The selected data give the same estimator distribution under both histories, so a universally unbiased estimate of their different totals is impossible. Capture the extra work through a separate draw or use a defensible range. |
| Predictions are fixed before the draw | Train on an earlier independent sample and condition the proof on the resulting predictions. A design with independent draws in disjoint groups can train each group's predictor using the other draws, then correct against its own draw. Conditional on the other groups' data, that predictor is fixed for the group it predicts. Summing the group estimates preserves unbiasedness. Precision calculations must allow for dependencies induced by shared training. Reusing the correction sample without such a design still requires a separate proof. |
| Every selected interval is measured accurately | For initial non-response, randomly select a follow-up sample with known positive conditional probabilities. Double weighting recovers the missing contribution in expectation if follow-up measures the selected intervals accurately. For unresolved or inaccurately timed intervals, propagate defensible lower and upper values through the weighted estimate. Those endpoints enclose the estimate that would result from the same draw with accurate measurements. To cover the fixed total, add uncertainty justified under the full sampling design. A sample with too little audit evidence to constrain systematic undercounting leaves that bias unresolved. |
| Precision improves when predictions reduce residual variation | Under simple random sampling without replacement the same factor multiplies both variance expressions. The comparison reduces to the spread of the hours and the spread of the prediction errors. For other designs, compare the variance of the weighted sums under that design. First-order probabilities suffice for unbiased totals. When a required pair cannot appear together, either change the draw to give the pair positive probability or use a variance bound. Independence between all selections is unnecessary. |
| Output per person-hour requires positive labour | Preserve the feasible pairs of output and labour when bounding a rate. With a minimum target rate $p$, minimise $Q-pH$ over those pairs. A positive minimum establishes $Q/H>p$ for all feasible pairs with positive labour. The calculation avoids dividing noisy estimates. A rate at zero labour remains undefined, so give both totals and indicate whether zero is feasible. If output stays positive as positive labour approaches zero, there is no finite upper rate to recover. For sampled outcomes the linear contrast needs its own confidence bound under the actual draw. |
| Forecast ranking assumes a suitable reference | Compare forecasts on the same subsequent tasks using information available at intake. When true hours lie in an audit range, the difference of squared errors between predictions $f$ and $g$ equals $(f-g)(f+g-2H)$, which is linear in actual labour $H$. Evaluating both endpoints bounds the comparison without assuming the midpoint is correct. A ranking that survives both endpoints is robust to the stated measurement uncertainty. The historical forecast itself still requires validation on incoming work. New task types and changes in review can alter its predictive performance. |

## Paired trial with incomplete outcomes

A trial can avoid the requirement for complete outcomes in every pair. Before assignment, specify a finite range $[a_i,b_i]$ for the true AI-minus-comparison difference $D_i$ in each pair $i$, for each outcome. Each retention fraction lies between zero and one, so its difference lies between minus one and one. For labour, a finite bound requires an evidenced limit on all eligible contributors' hours and positive initial reference scores known before assignment. Use the assigned offer to define the comparison. Outcomes must depend only on the assignment in their own pair for the following concentration calculation. Identifying the effect of an offer on individual teams also requires the team outcomes under each offer to be well defined without changes in other teams' assignments. If interference crosses pairs, change the unit of randomisation or substantiate a different inference.

With independent fair allocation between pairs, $\tau=m^{-1}\sum_i \mathbb{E}[D_i]$ is the mean difference over the assignment distribution for the $m$ enrolled pairs. The $D_i$ need not have the same distribution. Hoeffding's inequality gives a one-sided error limit $\alpha$ using the radius

$$
r_\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{2m^2}\sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2}.
$$

For the realised assignment, write $[l_i,u_i]$ for an interval containing the true difference, allowing for missing outcomes and possible measurement error. A fully missing pair can use its prespecified range. If a team's outcome lies in $[A^-,A^+]$ and the comparison outcome in $[C^-,C^+]$, use $[A^- - C^+,A^+ - C^-]$. The upper confidence limit for labour is the mean of the $u_i$ plus $r_\alpha$. The lower limit for retention is the mean of the $l_i$ minus the corresponding radius. Each limit is at least as conservative as the one calculated from the true realised differences. When the audit ranges can fail to contain the true differences with simultaneous probability at most $\gamma$, the failure probability of each resulting limit is at most $\alpha+\gamma$.

Both limits must clear their respective thresholds. If either outcome falls short in truth, a false benefit declaration requires a false decision for that outcome. This gives the individual error bound for the combined decision without an assumption about dependence between labour and retention. The same argument allows unequal effects across teams. The measurements, time caps and assignment procedure still have to justify the limits. Wide ranges can make the decision uninformative. For example, a common range of $[-10,10]$ person-hours per initial unit and 48 pairs gives a radius of 3.92 person-hours per unit at $\alpha=0.025$, even with complete outcome measurements. A more precise method or a larger trial may be needed. The existing simulations evaluate a different decision rule, so assess detection and error frequencies again before using this one. [Trial assumptions and simulations](../study/ai-labour-causal/causal-study.tex) · [Hoeffding's inequality](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830)

Under the original all-complete rule, the probability of declaring a benefit cannot exceed the probability of complete measurement. The formula $(1-q)^m$ requires independent pair losses with the same probability $q$. If the individual loss probabilities $q_i$ are known under arbitrary dependence, the probability of complete measurement lies between $\max(0,1-\sum_i q_i)$ and $1-\max_i q_i$. A shared outage illustrates why the difference matters. If every pair loses its outcome in the same outage of probability $q$, complete measurement has probability $1-q$. The six-pair pilot can diagnose such a mechanism and test the feasibility of independent audits. It cannot estimate rare-loss probabilities precisely or establish the effect of offering AI. A power calculation remains conditional on inputs the pilot can help substantiate. [Missing outcomes and pilot](../study/ai-labour-causal/causal-study.tex#L20-L24)
