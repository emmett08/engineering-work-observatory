# How the conclusions depend on the observations and assumptions

A limit on each person’s available hours bounds the time a team could have spent on a task. Restrictions on switching tasks can make some totals inside those bounds unattainable. Extending the bounds to contributors outside the team requires evidence about how many hours they could have worked. The last section uses comparable limits on unmeasured trial outcomes to test whether the evidence supports a benefit from AI access.

## Identifying labour

| Condition | What the evidence permits |
| --- | --- |
| Commit times identify work only under additional restrictions | Use brief independent observations of human activity, including times with no commit, to distinguish commit propensity from time spent working. In the commit model, the commit rate is the activity rate multiplied by the conditional commit probability. An exact positive value for either factor determines the other. A probability sample of activity can estimate total hours directly if the sampling frame covers the work and observers measure active time accurately. Where automation can produce commits or the tasks change, observe human activity during periods with and without commits. |
| The total refers to a particular group of people and work | For each person who may have contributed, allow their unaccounted time to include eligible work, other work or time spent idle, subject to their available hours. Include contributors outside that group if independent observations bound their combined hours. With no evidence limiting their number or available time, even a perfectly measured team total gives no finite upper limit for all contributors. |
| Exact allocation assumes freedom to switch activities within each interval between successive time boundaries | The original minimum and maximum enclose every total attainable under additional scheduling restrictions if the observed totals and time budgets still hold. The endpoints may cease to be attainable, and feasible totals can have gaps. If one hour of work in a two-hour period must run entirely before or entirely after the midpoint, the first-hour totals are zero and one. The interval from zero to one still encloses both possibilities. For restrictions that depend only on the simultaneous activities allowed within a cell, assign a nonnegative duration to each permitted combination and require the durations to sum to the cell length. Each allocation gives such durations, and placing the combinations in successive blocks gives the converse when combinations may switch freely. Minimum block lengths and dependencies across cells require separate scheduling constraints. |
| The measured totals and time limits contain the truth | Include bounds on rounding and independently measured observation errors in the constraints. For input limits estimated from a probability sample, set a failure allowance for each limit within the total error budget. The union bound then guarantees simultaneous coverage at the chosen level without assuming independent measurement errors. The guarantee still depends on defensible limits for bias and on coverage of the actual work. An infeasible problem means the supplied observations and restrictions conflict. |
| The retained score depends on which requirements were accepted and still work | Freeze the cohort and initial scoring rules before treatment. If reviewers cannot check a requirement worth $w$ initial units at follow-up, carry its possible retained score as $[0,w]$. A verified failure of one criterion or an independently confirmed late acceptance can tighten this range. Under this outcome definition, permanent discontinuation scores zero. The team must decide in advance which behaviour qualifies as fulfilment. |

## Sampling and comparing rates

| Condition | What the evidence permits |
| --- | --- |
| Every eligible interval has a known positive probability of selection | Publish the draw rule and check its coverage against the people and periods in the target. Treat work with zero selection probability as a separate unknown contribution. Consider two allocations of work that agree on every selectable interval and differ only on an excluded interval. Every possible draw looks the same under both allocations. An estimate based on the draw therefore has the same expectation for both and cannot be unbiased for two different totals. Sample the excluded intervals separately or bound their contribution from other evidence. |
| Predictions are fixed before the draw | Train on an earlier independent sample and condition the proof on the resulting predictions. A design with independent draws in disjoint groups can train each group's predictor using the other draws, then correct against its own draw. Conditional on the other groups' data, that predictor is fixed for the group it predicts. Summing the group estimates preserves unbiasedness. Precision calculations must allow for dependencies induced by shared training. Reusing the correction sample without such a design still requires a separate proof. |
| Every selected interval is measured accurately | For initial non-response, randomly select a follow-up sample with known positive conditional probabilities. Double weighting recovers the contribution from initial non-respondents in expectation if follow-up measures the selected intervals accurately. For unresolved or inaccurately timed intervals, propagate defensible lower and upper values through the weighted estimate. Those endpoints enclose the estimate that would result from the same draw with accurate measurements. To cover the fixed total, add uncertainty justified under the full sampling design. Systematic undercounting remains possible unless independent observations establish how many hours people may have omitted. |
| Precision improves when predictions reduce residual variation | Under simple random sampling without replacement, the same factor multiplies both variance expressions. When the sample covers only part of the population, prediction improves precision exactly when the finite-population variance of the errors is smaller than that of the hours. For other designs, compare the variance of the weighted sums under that design. First-order probabilities suffice for unbiased totals. When a required pair cannot appear together, either change the draw to give the pair positive probability or use a variance bound. |
| Output per person-hour requires positive labour | Preserve the feasible pairs of output and labour when bounding a rate. With a minimum target rate $p$, minimise $Q-pH$ over those pairs. A positive minimum establishes $Q/H>p$ for all feasible pairs with positive labour. A rate at zero labour remains undefined, so give both totals and indicate whether zero is feasible. If output stays bounded away from zero along feasible pairs whose positive labour approaches zero, there is no finite upper rate to recover. For sampled outcomes the linear contrast needs its own confidence bound under the actual draw. |
| Forecast ranking assumes a suitable reference | Compare forecasts on the same subsequent tasks using information available at intake. When independent measurements bound the true hours, the difference of squared errors between predictions $f$ and $g$ equals $(f-g)(f+g-2H)$, which is linear in actual labour $H$. Evaluating both endpoints bounds the comparison without assuming the midpoint is correct. If the difference has the same strict sign at both endpoints, the same forecast has the smaller squared error throughout the interval. Test the forecast derived from earlier tasks on incoming work because a new task type or a change in review practice can affect its accuracy. |

## Paired trial with incomplete outcomes

A pair with incomplete measurements can still contribute a range of possible differences between groups. Before assignment, specify a finite range $[a_i,b_i]$ for the true AI-minus-comparison difference $D_i$ in each pair $i$, for each outcome. Each retention fraction lies between zero and one, so its difference lies between minus one and one. For labour, a finite bound requires an evidenced limit on all eligible contributors' hours and positive initial reference scores known before assignment. Use the assigned offer to define the comparison. Outcomes must depend only on the assignment in their own pair for the following concentration calculation. Identifying the effect of an offer on individual teams also requires the team outcomes under each offer to be well defined without changes in other teams' assignments. If one pair’s assignment affects outcomes in another, use a unit of randomisation that contains those interactions or derive uncertainty limits that allow for them.

With an independent fair draw in each pair, $\tau=m^{-1}\sum_i \mathbb{E}[D_i]$ is the mean difference over the assignment distribution for the $m$ enrolled pairs. The $D_i$ need not have the same distribution. Hoeffding's inequality gives a one-sided error limit $\alpha$ using the radius

$$
r_\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{2m^2}\sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2}.
$$

For the realised assignment, write $[l_i,u_i]$ for an interval containing the true difference, allowing for unobserved outcomes and possible measurement error. Use the pair’s prespecified range if both teams lack the outcome. If a team's outcome lies in $[A^-,A^+]$ and the comparison outcome in $[C^-,C^+]$, use $[A^- - C^+,A^+ - C^-]$. The upper confidence limit for labour is the mean of the $u_i$ plus $r_\alpha$. The lower limit for retention is the mean of the $l_i$ minus the corresponding radius. Each limit is at least as conservative as the one calculated from the true realised differences. When the probability that any measurement interval excludes its true difference is at most $\gamma$, the failure probability of each resulting limit is at most $\alpha+\gamma$.

Both limits must clear their respective thresholds. If either outcome falls short in truth, a false benefit declaration requires a false decision for that outcome. The individual error bound therefore covers the combined decision even when labour and retention are dependent or effects differ across teams. Wide ranges of possible outcomes can leave the trial unable to resolve a benefit. For example, a common range of $[-10,10]$ person-hours per initial unit across 48 pairs gives a radius of 3.92 person-hours per unit at $\alpha=0.025$, even with complete outcome measurements. A more precise method or a larger trial may be needed. Before adopting this rule, repeat the simulations with its thresholds to estimate how often it detects a real benefit and how often it declares one in error. [Trial assumptions and simulations](../study/ai-labour-causal/causal-study.tex) · [Hoeffding's inequality](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830)

Under the original all-complete rule, the probability of declaring a benefit cannot exceed the probability of complete measurement. The formula $(1-q)^m$ requires independent pair losses with the same probability $q$. If the individual loss probabilities $q_i$ are known under arbitrary dependence, the probability of complete measurement lies between $\max(0,1-\sum_i q_i)$ and $1-\max_i q_i$. If a single outage with probability $q$ erases the outcomes of all pairs, complete measurement has probability $1-q$. The six-pair feasibility study can identify shared points of failure and test whether independent observers can check the measurements. Six pairs leave substantial uncertainty about rare losses and about the effect of offering AI. Use the feasibility results to choose plausible inputs for the power calculation and show how power varies over those inputs.
