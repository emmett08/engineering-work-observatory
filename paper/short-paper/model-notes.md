# How the conclusions depend on the observations and assumptions

A requirement specifies the behaviour the software must provide. Each task in this comparison has one scored requirement. A contributor can perform several activities on that task, such as implementation and review. The contributor's time spent on those activities counts towards the task's labour during its labour window. If one contributor divides an hour between two tasks, the two durations sum to at most one person-hour.

## Identifying labour

| Condition | What the evidence permits |
| --- | --- |
| Commit times identify work only under additional restrictions | Use brief independent observations of human activity, including times with no commit, to distinguish commit propensity from time spent working. In the commit model, the commit rate is the activity rate multiplied by the conditional commit probability. An exact positive value for either factor determines the other. Direct weighting estimates total hours if each eligible interval for each contributor has a known positive chance of selection and the supplied durations equal the time spent on the requirements. Where automation can produce commits or the tasks change, observe human activity during periods with and without commits. |
| The labour total covers specified tasks, people and periods | Include time a contributor spends carrying out or supporting an included task during its labour window. For a target covering all contributors, an external review qualifies under the same rule as implementation by a team member. For hours whose use is unknown, allow every activity consistent with the task, the contributor’s availability and the restrictions in force. A verified absence rules out work by that contributor during the absence. A gap in the observations leaves the duration uncertain. To extend a measured team total to other contributors, obtain evidence about their combined hours. Without a limit on their number or availability, the larger total has no finite upper bound. |
| Contributors can switch activities within each time cell | When switching is unrestricted, every total between the computed minimum and maximum has a feasible allocation of hours. A minimum session length or a break in availability can exclude some allocations. For example, a continuous one-hour review must fit entirely on one side of a break between two one-hour periods. The review contributes zero hours or one hour to the first period. To model activities that must coincide, such as two reviewers working together, give each permitted combination a nonnegative duration. The durations sum to the cell length. If the team can switch combinations freely within the cell, successive blocks of those lengths use exactly the calculated hours. Minimum block lengths and dependencies across cells require additional constraints. |
| The observations and available time permit the hours actually worked | For a rounded duration, include every duration that would round to the value supplied. Allow for further errors, including systematic undercounting, when independent checks bound their size. For a range estimated from a sample, bound the probability that it excludes the hours worked. If these probabilities sum to at most $\alpha_{\mathrm{obs}}$, the union bound gives probability at least $1-\alpha_{\mathrm{obs}}$ that all ranges contain the corresponding hours. The resulting bounds then cover labour for the people and periods included, provided the actual activities obey the time constraints. An empty feasible set means that the evidence and restrictions conflict. |
| The retained score depends on first acceptance and the behaviour seen at follow-up | Fix each task's requirement and scoring rule before the AI offer. If reviewers cannot assess the requirement for a task $i$ accepted by the cutoff, its possible retained score runs from zero to its initial weight $s_i$. If a weighted check fails, its share of the score is lost. A task first accepted after the cutoff scores zero. Defining in advance which behaviour satisfies each criterion makes it possible to apply the same rule in both groups. |

## Sampling and comparing rates

| Condition | What the evidence permits |
| --- | --- |
| Every interval in the sampling population has a known positive chance of selection | The population $\mathcal I$ contains the person-intervals for the contributors and periods in scope, including intervals in which they did no work on an included task. Sampling estimates $H_{\mathcal I}$, the labour in those intervals. The team's full-window total $H=\sum_i H_i$ includes any eligible hours outside those intervals. The totals coincide when the sampling population covers all the contributors and time under comparison and counts their eligible hours once. Two allocations that agree on every selectable interval give the same distribution of sampled hours. If they differ on an interval outside the draw, no estimate based on the draw alone can be unbiased for both larger totals. Sample the excluded intervals separately or bound their contribution using other evidence. |
| Predictions are fixed before the draw | Train on an earlier independent sample and condition the proof on the resulting predictions. A design with independent draws in disjoint groups can train each group's predictor using the other draws, then correct against its own draw. Conditional on the other groups' data, that predictor is fixed for the group it predicts. Summing the group estimates preserves unbiasedness. Precision calculations must allow for dependencies induced by shared training. Reusing the correction sample without such a design still requires a separate proof. |
| Each duration measures a person's time spent on the included tasks | Draw a follow-up sample from intervals for which contributors initially supplied no duration. Every such interval needs a known positive chance of selection. Obtain the hours worked in each interval drawn at follow-up and divide its prediction error by the product of its two selection probabilities. The expected correction then includes all intervals lacking an initial duration. If a sampled interval's hours are uncertain, use the smallest possible duration in each term of the weighted estimate to bound it below and the largest to bound it above. These bounds enclose the estimate for that draw whenever the range for each interval contains its actual hours. To cover $H_{\mathcal I}$, also allow for variation across draws. |
| Precision improves when predictions reduce residual variation | Under simple random sampling without replacement, the same factor multiplies both variance expressions. When the sample covers only part of the population, prediction improves precision exactly when the finite-population variance of the errors is smaller than that of the hours. For other designs, compare the variance of the weighted sums under that design. First-order probabilities suffice for unbiased totals. When a required pair cannot appear together, either change the draw to give the pair positive probability or use a variance bound. |
| Retained score per person-hour requires positive labour | Let $Q$ be the total retained score and $H$ the corresponding full-window labour. For a proposed rate $\rho$, minimise $Q-\rho H$ over the feasible pairs of score and labour. A positive minimum establishes $Q/H>\rho$ for every such pair with positive labour. At zero labour give both totals. If the retained score stays bounded away from zero as feasible positive labour approaches zero, the rate has no finite upper bound. When sampling supplies either total, the difference $Q-\rho H$ needs a confidence bound under that sampling design. |
| Forecasts use only information available when the task enters the comparison | Compare two forecasts, $\phi_i$ and $\psi_i$, of the same task's full-window labour $H_i$. An earlier task can supply a labour value only if its window has closed and measured hours are available by the new task's entry. The first forecast's squared error minus the second's is $(\phi_i-\psi_i)(\phi_i+\psi_i-2H_i)$. This expression is linear in $H_i$. Evaluate it at the smallest and largest labour values the evidence permits. If both results are strictly negative, the first forecast has smaller squared error throughout the range. If both are positive, the second does. Test the forecasts on incoming tasks because a change in task type or review practice can affect accuracy. |

## Paired trial with incomplete outcomes

For each team let $S=\sum_i s_i>0$ be the total initial weight of its requirements. Index the two outcomes by $o\in\{\mathrm{lab},\mathrm{ret}\}$. The labour outcome is $H/S$ and the retention outcome is $Q/S$. In pair $h$, subtract the comparison team's outcome from that of the team offered AI, giving the difference $D_{ho}$. Before the draw, fix a finite range $[a_{ho},b_{ho}]$ for this difference that covers either choice of team to receive the offer. A retention difference lies between minus one and one. For labour, use each team's initial score and a cap on the hours all its contributors could spend on its included tasks.

Let $P$ be the number of pairs. The independent fair draws determine both the observed pair differences and their mean $\overline D_o=P^{-1}\sum_{h=1}^{P}D_{ho}$. Its expectation over possible draws is $\mu_o$. With the teams' potential outcomes fixed, a pair difference depends only on its own draw if the offer in one pair has no effect on another pair. Independent draws then make the pair differences independent. They can have different distributions. For the effect on individual teams, interpretation also requires each team's outcomes to depend only on its own offer. If outcomes depend on another pair’s draw, account for that dependence or randomise groups large enough to contain the interactions.

At error probability $0<\alpha_o<1$, Hoeffding's inequality bounds the chance that $\overline D_o$ falls more than $\varepsilon_o$ below $\mu_o$ by $\alpha_o$. The same probability bound applies above $\mu_o$. The margin is

$$
\varepsilon_o=\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\alpha_o)}{2P^2}\sum_{h=1}^{P}(b_{ho}-a_{ho})^2}.
$$

After the draw, bound each $D_{ho}$ with the measurements available for that pair and outcome. If both team outcomes are uncertain, subtract the largest possible comparison value from the smallest possible AI-team value to obtain the lower bound $\ell_{ho}$. Subtract the smallest possible comparison value from the largest possible AI-team value for the upper bound $u_{ho}$. If both values are unavailable, use the range fixed before the draw. For labour, add the labour margin to the mean of the upper bounds. For retention, subtract the retention margin from the mean of the lower bounds.

For outcome $o$, let $\gamma_o$ bound the probability that any pair's range excludes $D_{ho}$. The corresponding one-sided confidence limit then excludes $\mu_o$ with probability at most $\alpha_o+\gamma_o$. If the true effect fails the decision threshold for that outcome, a false benefit declaration requires its confidence limit to exclude the true effect. The probability of a false declaration is therefore at most $\max_o(\alpha_o+\gamma_o)$ whenever at least one effect fails its threshold. This bound allows dependence between the two outcomes.

For 48 pairs with a labour difference between $-10$ and $10$ person-hours per initial unit in every pair, $\alpha_{\mathrm{lab}}=0.025$ gives a margin of about 3.92 person-hours per unit. Even with exact labour measurements, the observed mean saving must exceed about 4.92 person-hours per unit to establish a saving above one. Simulate this rule across plausible effects and measurement errors to estimate how often it detects a worthwhile benefit or declares one incorrectly. [Trial assumptions and simulations](../study/ai-labour-causal/causal-study.tex) · [Hoeffding's inequality](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830)

The original trial rule requires both outcomes from every team. If pairs independently lack at least one outcome with common probability $q$, all $P$ pairs supply both outcomes with probability $(1-q)^P$. With pair-specific loss probabilities $q_h$ and arbitrary dependence, the probability of complete measurement lies between $\max(0,1-\sum_{h=1}^{P}q_h)$ and $1-\max_h q_h$. If a single outage with probability $q$ erases the outcomes of all pairs, complete measurement has probability $1-q$. A preliminary comparison with six pairs can identify shared causes of loss and check whether independent observers can establish the hours and scores used by the trial. Six pairs leave substantial uncertainty about rare losses and the effect of the offer. Use those observations to choose plausible inputs for the power calculation and show how power varies over them.
